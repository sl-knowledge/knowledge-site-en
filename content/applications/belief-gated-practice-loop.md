## One-page guide

Why might similar principles for living produce different effects when read in a book and when embedded in a community's sustained practice? This article connects entry into religious participation, community support, commitment, lived experience and attribution in a candidate loop:

**Entry through belief, relationships or habits → interaction between investment and practice structures → outcomes → attribution of those outcomes → beliefs, needs and investment in the next round.**

Belief may be a starting point or develop after participation. Which needs matter to an agent, and whether a premise is considered irreplaceable, can influence choices within the loop; practice may also change needs and beliefs. Outcomes may help or burden participants. Whether an experience is real, whether its causal attribution is justified, and whether a belief premise is true must be checked separately. None proves the others.

The condensation achieved here is a shared representation for tracing temporal order, feedback and alternative explanations across questions previously discussed as entry, organisation, experience and belief. It supplies research propositions and a record card. It does not claim to explain all religion or infer an individual's life history from group-level patterns.

## 1. Similar content, potentially different effects

Many principles in religious texts—restraint, forgiveness, treating others well and valuing relationships over wealth—also appear elsewhere. If religion's role were only to transmit this content, buying a book could replace it.

Knowing a principle, however, does not mean being able to practise it over time. A religious community can sometimes turn ideas into stable practices where individual reading may not. The question is: **why might similar content produce different outcomes in different practice structures?** This cannot begin by assuming that religious outcomes are necessarily better or that faith has already been established as their sole cause.

## 2. Generative rule: a five-part loop

The symbols below are local to this article: C denotes investment, S practice structures and A attribution. They cannot be substituted directly for capability, buffers or agents denoted by the same letters elsewhere. Empirical research must specify how each is recorded or measured.

Let:

- **N (Need):** a need or goal important to the agent, such as belonging, comfort, meaning, justice or eternal life.
- **P (Premise):** an accepted core premise, such as the existence and authority of a transcendent being.
- **X (Exclusivity):** the degree to which the agent considers P irreplaceable for achieving N.
- **C:** investment through learning, observance and participation.
- **S:** structures of constraint and reinforcement: rules, mutual support and monitoring, gatherings, ritual, identity and exit costs.
- **Y:** experienced or observable outcomes: behavioural change, support, comfort and belonging, potentially including burdens or harm.
- **A:** attribution: how the agent explains the source of Y, whether through P, practice, relationships or several causes.

The minimal practice loop is:

```text
Entry (belief P / relationships / practice habits)
                       ↓
       C (investment) ↔ S (practice structures)
                       ↓
           Y (outcomes, possibly delayed by τ)
                       ↓
          A (interpretation and attribution)
                       ↓
       Revise P, N, identity and later investment C
                       ↺
```

Three structural features matter:

1. **Investment precedes some benefits.** Some outcomes arrive only after a delay τ, leaving no immediately verifiable personal return. Accepting P need not be the only entry: family transmission, close relationships or prior participation can initiate C. P may begin the loop or emerge from it.
2. **S may help turn one-time agreement into sustained practice.** Rules, community support, regular routines, ritual and identity can change behaviour and experience. Transferring words without their practice conditions may weaken effects. This does not establish S as the sole or principal cause of all religious outcomes.
3. **Attribution affects the next round.** Someone who receives genuine help may attribute it to P and invest further, or to community support or personal effort. **The reality of experience, the explanation of its cause and the truth of P are three different questions.** Positive feedback can also reinforce harmful outcomes or mistaken attributions; Y cannot be assumed positive in advance.

The loop has two levels:

- **Individual:** belief in ultimate authority, finding meaning or accepting practice disciplines → changes in behaviour and experience → possible further changes in identity, investment and belief.
- **Group:** mutual support, monitoring and reinforcement → some participants remain → their visible experiences may attract newcomers. Those who leave must also be observed to avoid survivorship bias.

### 2.1 One possible source of P: need N and perceived irreplaceability X

Placing P at the beginning still leaves unexplained **why an agent is willing to believe or maintain P**. What important need does it promise to meet? Does the agent perceive other feasible paths?

Eternal life illustrates two different arguments:

- **An argument about truth:** “I want eternal life; I think only God can grant it; **therefore God must exist**.” The conclusion does not follow logically.
- **An argument about belief formation:** “I strongly want eternal life; I believe it may be achievable if P is true; I see no acceptable alternative; therefore I may be more motivated to accept or maintain P.” This describes a psychological and social mechanism, not a proof of God's existence.

A high **N** does not automatically produce belief in **P**. **X** also matters: does the agent regard P as the only path to N? When N is important and X high, information against P may imply a large perceived loss and encourage defence of P. Acceptable alternatives may change the effect. This is a **testable hypothesis about a tendency**, not a universal law; perceived loss is not factual proof.

The same faith need not be driven by the same need. Reunion with loved ones, justice, meaning, belonging and an orderly life may matter differently. Needs can coexist and change with circumstances. Causation may also run the other way: family or religious education can form P first and subsequently shape which needs matter. The more general structure is **N ↔ P**, not “need must precede premise”.

### 2.2 What may change after understanding the mechanism?

Growing awareness of S, Y and A may change attribution. Some leave, some continue while suspending particular truth judgements, and others retain P after reflection. **Understanding the mechanism does not itself require leaving the loop**, nor does it negate sincerity. The research question is how mechanism awareness changes evidence updating and practice choices, rather than deciding anyone's faith for them.

## 3. Research propositions and a record card

1. **Practice structures:** with similar content, differences in sustained practice, relationships and community support can explain some differences in behaviour and life outcomes. *Potential disconfirmation:* after controlling prior differences, the strength of S has no stable association with outcomes.
2. **Load-bearing premises:** in some groups, P and X can strengthen long-term commitment and thereby improve organisational persistence and cooperation. *Potential disconfirmation:* groups without P, with comparable participation intensity and resources, perform no worse than groups with P.
3. **Entry conditions:** combinations of security, transmission structures, turning points, alternatives and gate strength can predict different patterns of conversion and participation. *Potential disconfirmation:* predefined combinations fail to predict entry distributions across societies.
4. **Convergence:** ultimate promises whose deadlines can be reinterpreted may persist more readily than rigid, time-bound promises that have failed. *Potential disconfirmation:* comparable samples show no such persistence difference.
5. **Transition channels:** when belief is deeply linked to relationships, identity and high-value needs, non-argumentative factors may matter more for entry and exit. *Potential disconfirmation:* after controlling these factors, propositional argument still dominates most transitions.
6. **Need and irreplaceability:** other conditions being similar, greater importance N and perceived irreplaceability X may reduce the effect of contrary evidence on P. *Potential disconfirmation:* N and X do not predict evidence weighting in predefined belief-updating tasks.
7. **Mechanism awareness:** increasing awareness M may change attribution A without necessarily weakening P. *Potential disconfirmation:* longitudinal changes in M are entirely unrelated to attribution or participation patterns.

These are research propositions, not completed validations. Designs must distinguish self-selection, prior belief, group support and temporal order. Correlation cannot simply be treated as causation.

### A practice-loop record card

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Entry and temporal order | Which came first: belief, relationships, transmission or practice? What is the evidence? |
| Needs and alternatives | What needs did the participant express? Was the premise considered irreplaceable? List unknowns separately. |
| Actual investment and structures | What was done? What support, constraints or exit costs were involved? |
| Outcomes and comparison baseline | Which are subjective experiences, which observable changes? Compared with what baseline? |
| Attribution and the next round | How were outcomes explained? How did belief, needs or participation subsequently change? |
| Rival explanations and disconfirmation | Could self-selection, prior relationships, concurrent events or other support explain the change? What would weaken the original account? |

The card is a research record. External observation alone does not establish another person's actual motives.

## 4. An inventory of patterns

The following provides comparison targets, avoiding the assumption that one form of participation is the only form.

| Dimension | Patterns |
|---|---|
| Entry requirements | **Belief first:** accepting doctrine precedes membership. **Practice first:** participation in ritual and norms precedes or takes priority over belief. |
| Entry channels | Inherited participation; relationship-based entry before belief; entry through crisis or migration; entry through persuasion. |
| Distribution across time and place | Decline in some societies and expansion in others; different trajectories for the same tradition. |
| Other organisations for comparison | Secular communes, gatherings without religious premises and mutual-support groups: compare support, discipline and commitment without assuming they reproduce the same structure minus belief. |
| Internal experience | Some members report improvement, comfort and belonging; explanations of the causes may differ. |
| External impressions | Some outsiders first question the premise and may underestimate effects of practice and relationships. |
| Ultimate promises | Some have deadlines and others do not; failure may produce dissolution, reinterpretation or further reinforcement. |

These are explanatory targets, not an exhaustive classification. A candidate mechanism must specify which patterns it explains under which conditions and where other mechanisms are needed. Assigning parameters to a pattern is not itself an explanation.

## 5. How a premise may bear load

One tempting simplification is that religion is merely a behavioural organisation dressed in transcendence: remove the outer layer and retain the organisation and rules for the same effects. **That generalisation does not hold and is not this article's conclusion.**

Historical comparisons of religious and secular communes found longer persistence for the former in particular samples (Sosis & Bressler, 2003, reference 3). This suggests that transcendent belief may matter for commitment and cooperation, but does not establish that P is irreplaceable in every setting. Meaning, awe, comfort, ritual and a relationship with transcendence are not merely accessories to behavioural discipline.

A candidate mechanism is: **when P is regarded as ultimately authoritative and X is high, P may support more stable commitment to S.** If rules are understood as coming from an authority not open to ordinary negotiation, an agent may invest more. P may then be an important slow variable. Whether it is the most important load-bearing variable depends on the tradition and evidence, rather than being stipulated beforehand.

This requires a distinction:

> **Some practical effects can be observed, while some ultimate promises cannot be tested in the same way. Neither proves the other.**

Behaviour and life outcomes can be studied to examine the benefits of practices. The truth of ultimate promises lies beyond direct assessment by that same set of social-scientific measures. Practical help neither automatically proves P nor does identifying a social mechanism alone disprove it.

## 6. Ultimate promises and deadlines: a possible selection mechanism

A promise with a specific deadline faces a test when the deadline arrives. Failure may lead to dissolution, reinterpretation or continued participation through new forms of mobilisation.

A possible selection process is that **promises without specific deadlines, or with reinterpretable deadlines, may be less exposed to the direct impact of a single failure.** Some groups dissolve after failed prophecy, some intensify mobilisation and others reinterpret it. This convergence hypothesis needs testing across traditions and periods; it cannot assume all long-lived religions necessarily converge on one form.

## 7. Entry and the “walled city” analogy

### 7.1 An asymmetry

| | Inside the loop | Outside the loop |
|---|---|---|
| Outcomes | May receive practical, relational or meaning-related help, and may incur costs | May not see some internal experiences, or may receive similar help through other paths |
| Explanation | May attribute outcomes to P or also consider organisational, behavioural and psychological mechanisms | May understand some mechanisms without the same firsthand experience |
| Attitude to P | Y may strengthen P, or P may be reinterpreted | Tension between P and existing knowledge may discourage entry |

In **strongly belief-first** systems, P can be a **binding constraint** for some outsiders: inability to accept it prevents entry into a particular participation path. In practice-first systems, participation before belief is possible; outsiders may also receive similar help through secular groups. For established participants, the important constraint may shift towards continuing investment, identity relationships and exit costs.

### 7.2 Entry channels

Entry is not limited to being persuaded by argument:

1. **Transmission:** learning and participation begin before active examination of faith; later acceptance, revision or rejection may follow.
2. **Relationships:** personal connections and group acceptance may precede gradual acceptance of doctrine—**belonging before believing**.
3. **Turning points:** migration, bereavement, illness, unemployment or loneliness can interrupt support, and an existing community may fill the gap.
4. **Argument:** assess the premise as credible before investing. This route may be difficult for those requiring strong evidence and unwilling to participate first.

### 7.3 Why argument alone may be insufficient

If belief develops mainly through relationships, life events and prolonged experience, a single argument may not produce entry or exit. What would have to change includes identity, support networks and expectations, not just a proposition. A weaker, more testable claim follows:

> **When belief is deeply linked to identity, relationships and high-value needs, propositional argument alone may have less influence on belief transitions.**

Argument is not ineffective: for some, it can be decisive. Believers may also explain their histories clearly; inability to articulate them cannot be assumed universal.

## 8. Analytical conditions and possible patterns

The fields below concern individual states, group structures and social environments. A field should be treated as a parameter only when approximately stable within a stated research horizon. They are not all fixed or measured on a shared scale.

| Field | Meaning | Candidate role in the loop |
|---|---|---|
| $\theta_{1}$ Gate strength | Priority given to belief | Accepting P may increasingly become necessary for formal entry; participation and membership must still be distinguished |
| $\theta_{2}$ Premise tension | Tension between P and the agent's existing knowledge and evidential standards | Greater tension may raise acceptance barriers in argument-based entry |
| $\theta_{3}$ Existential security | Stability of social protection, healthcare and public order | May reduce some crisis-related support needs, without implying that meaning, relationships or religious participation must diminish |
| $\theta_{4}$ Transmission strength | Conditions of transmission in families, intergenerational life and communities | May increase early exposure and participation, without guaranteeing later belief or retention |
| $\theta_{5}$ Turning-point frequency | Frequency of relevant life transitions in the population studied | May increase use of particular entry channels when support, relationships and participation opportunities are available |
| $\theta_{6}$ Alternative-loop availability | Secular communities, psychological services and public welfare | May increase acceptable alternatives, without presuming existing faith loses meaning |
| $\theta_{7}$ Structural strictness | Rule density, monitoring and member costs | May increase retention and investment, and may also raise exit costs |
| **$\theta_{8}$ Need importance N** | Importance of a need to the agent | May influence investment and which outcomes Y matter |
| **$\theta_{9}$ Perceived irreplaceability X** | Whether the agent thinks N requires P | Higher values may raise the perceived loss if P fails, without making P truer |
| **$\theta_{10}$ Mechanism awareness M** | Understanding of S, Y and A | May change attribution and participation, with no direction assumed |
| τ Outcome delay | Time from investment to visible outcomes | Longer delays may require commitment, support or early feedback to bridge the wait |

These conditional expectations support comparison; they do not replace evidence across settings:

- **High $\theta_{3}$, weak $\theta_{4}$, high $\theta_{2}$ and high $\theta_{1}$:** with comparable resources, organisation and opportunities for transmission, crisis, inherited or argumentative routes may each be restricted. This does not imply that all routes close or members must age without replacement.
- **Low $\theta_{3}$, high $\theta_{5}$ and low $\theta_{6}$:** if a group supplies relevant support, turning-point or relationship routes may become more attractive. High premise tension can still prevent some entry; expansion is not inevitable.
- **Low $\theta_{1}$ (practice first):** premise tension may restrict some participation routes less, but continued customs, routines and identity still depend on practical conditions.
- **High $\theta_{7}$:** strictness may select out low-investment members, raising average investment among those retained, while also increasing unreasonable retention pressure.
- **High N and high X:** the agent may value P's promise more and find contrary information harder to accept, depending also on evidential standards and alternatives.
- **High N and low X:** a strong need may be met through other beliefs, communities or secular arrangements, without maintaining the same P.
- **High M:** reflective belief, suspended judgement, modified practice or exit may result; no single direction is presumed.

**Mapping needs to predominant outcomes Y** is also hypothetical: belonging may focus attention on connection, justice on moral order, and hope for eternal life on ultimate promises. Needs may coexist, and P or the community may change their ordering.

Blocked entry is therefore not a universal feature of “modern people”, but one possible pattern arising from several conditions. No parameter combination guarantees the same response from everyone.

## 9. The other side of the mechanism

Positive feedback has no intrinsic moral direction. Forces making habits stable and mutual support reliable may also make doubt harder to express and exit more difficult. Rules, identity and relationships may support or pressure participants. Stronger feedback does not alone mean better outcomes.

Likewise, **understanding a mechanism does not require disbelief**. Someone may understand how community, ritual and practice change life while sincerely accepting a transcendent premise, or retain practices while suspending judgement about that premise. The article distinguishes what happened, why it happened and how participants understand it; it does not decide anyone's faith.

## 10. Candidate transfers across domains

Replace “transcendent premise” with **a premise or path thought to require commitment before benefits become visible**, and similar loops may occur elsewhere. The proposed transfer concerns structure, not equivalent truth judgements:

- **Long-term training and recovery programmes:** τ may be long and early returns absent. Study how knowledge, early feedback, coaches or peers support continued investment; tasks need not share one commitment structure.
- **Organisational culture and mission:** record mission P, institutions S, results Y and attribution A separately, then test whether they form a reinforcing loop. Naming them does not establish one.
- **Collective belief and organisational participation:** compare entry requirements, belonging, attribution and exit conditions without inferring one mechanism from similar appearances.
- **Education and self-study:** knowing a method is not mastering it. Verifiable incremental progress, practice feedback and support may reduce reliance on absolute initial acceptance of a premise.
- **Long-term organisational commitment:** agents who regard a path as the sole route to an important goal (high N, high X) may defend it. Credible alternatives may change that constraint, while strategic facts still require independent checking.

This raises an open design question:

> **Can a loop achieve comparable commitment without relying on unverifiable premises?**

Possible sources include public commitments, peer support, identity and verifiable incremental delivery. Whether these sustain effective investment while reducing unnecessary dependence and exit costs remains a research question.

The proposed contribution is not discovery of any component. It is their connection in **a parameterisable practice loop**: entry routes, needs that make premises important, the effect of outcomes on attribution, and patterns of persistence, transformation and exit. It remains a mechanism model requiring disconfirmation and comparison, not a conclusion about all religion.
