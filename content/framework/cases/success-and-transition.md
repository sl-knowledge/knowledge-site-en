> **Public edition v0.2 · Corporate history and mechanism hypotheses.** Based on *Early Success, Advance Preparation and Cross-Generation Reconstruction in Japanese Companies* v0.4 (23 October 2026), focused on Sony/Apple digital music. Primary announcements and research references checked 1 November 2026. The case proposes competing explanations; causal contributions remain untested.

## 1. What does success leave behind?

Sustained success accumulates two kinds of assets.

Cash, technology, brands, talent and customer/supplier relationships support new experiments and buffer trial and error.

Budget rules, departmental authority, performance standards, specific assets and commitments improve the old system but may make changing it expensive.

Success can therefore increase resources and reconstruction costs together. **After environmental change, can existing resources be reorganised into capabilities suited to the new market?**

"Organisational reconstruction" means observable changes: budget reallocation authority, joint decisions across departments, product/technology links, responsibility for experience and internal benefit allocation.

## 2. Sony and Apple: put facts on a timeline

| Date | Primary-source fact | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| January 2001 | Apple launches iTunes | A music-management product preceded that year's player |
| October 2001 | iPod announcement includes automatic Mac/iTunes synchronisation | Value includes device/software connections, not hardware alone |
| September 2005 | Sony's mid-term strategy seeks removal of business silos, concentrated decisions and unified software | Internal coordination was an acknowledged problem under active treatment |

Sources: [iTunes announcement](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2001/01/09Apple-Introduces-iTunes-Worlds-Best-and-Easiest-To-Use-Jukebox-Software/), [iPod announcement](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2001/10/23Apple-Presents-iPod/), [Sony strategy](https://www.sony.com/en/SonyInfo/News/Press/200509/05-050E/).

This opens the question of timely cross-business recombination in resource-rich firms. It does not identify organisational barriers' causal weight. Experience, components, price, rights, channels, competitors and market timing require separate checks.

## 3. Many resources, few executable paths

A company may own players, computers, content and hardware R&D without their functioning as one system.

A new product may require:

1. Software/hardware iterations around one experience;
2. Authority to move budgets/staff across businesses;
3. New benefit-sharing among content, devices and services;
4. Change within cash, contract and delivery constraints;
5. Effective products before competitors narrow the window.

Authority, coordination, commitments or combination time may bind. Identify concrete blocked actions before naming the constraint.

**Complete the capability combination customers need while profitable opportunity remains, preserving operating feasibility along the path.**

"Preparation before the window narrows" is diagnostic only. Windows move with customers, competitors, standards and supply chains, and may close for only one entry path.

## 4. Success and crisis each have two effects

Old-business income supports departments reinforcing old products/budgets. After change, old optimisation offers short-term returns while cross-department reconstruction imposes immediate losses.

Yet the same cash can finance new R&D and experimentation. Redeployable capital, clear interfaces and authority may advantage incumbents.

Crisis may remove support for old plans and enable reallocation, or exhaust cash, talent and trust before reform completes.

The original observation—Apple first faced difficulties while Sony had more successful businesses—becomes a question: **what were the returns to continuing old structures, costs of breaking them and resources needed to transform?** Outcomes alone establish neither beneficial crisis nor inevitable rigidity.

## 5. Competing explanations

| Explanation | Evidence sought | Weakening evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Old constraints block reconstruction | Opportunity recognised but budgets/authority/conflict block projects | Free resource transfer, failure for other reasons |
| Industry value capture shifts | Modularity, standards/platforms reduce retained technology profit | Internal execution explains differences |
| Starting/complementary capabilities differ | Prelaunch software, supply, customers and decisions differ | Similar preparation, persistent later divergence |
| Direction/experience is wrong | Reform occurs but bets, cost or positioning miss demand | Competitive products mainly internally blocked |

RIETI's digital-appliance research on modularity, intermediate markets and value capture suggests testing industry change alongside organisation. [Summary](https://www.rieti.go.jp/en/publications/summary/06030013.html)

## 6. Counterexamples belong in the text

Japanese firms differ. Hitachi's 2023 integrated report reviews portfolio reform, exits and capability acquisitions. Examine who changed what, rather than treating "Japanese companies" as one organisation type. [CEO account](https://www.hitachi.com/IR-e/library/integrated/online/2023/ceo_message.html)

Deepening materials, processes or components may also be sound specialisation. Judge targets, environment, returns and sustainable paths, not whether every firm becomes an end-user platform.

## 7. Making the case testable

Choose a date before the technological transition. Collect then-available plans, teams, software, suppliers, budgets, authority, cash and commitments. Set evaluation before outcomes.

Compare:

- **Across firms:** preparation and time to effective product in one window;
- **Within firms:** different businesses under similar rules;
- **Successes/failures:** successful reconstruction, successful deepening, fast wrong bets and resource-starved change.

If poorly prepared/misaligned pioneers repeatedly catch up while prepared entrants repeatedly fail, reduce explanatory weight on initial structure/speed.

## 8. Three company-research questions

1. Which capabilities can be redeployed, and which are fixed by contracts, authority or specific assets?
2. Does competition require deeper old capabilities or new business links/authority allocation?
3. Is a real change path available before resource exhaustion, customer lock-in and competitor progress?

Past success changes future choice conditions. Observe how conditions affect executable actions and whether feedback supports adjustment.
