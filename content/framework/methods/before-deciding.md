## Overview

Suppose a team is deciding whether to sign a year-long contract. The pros-and-cons list already covers price and service, but the discussion has stalled. Once someone asks what it would cost to exit early, the team realises it could also ask the supplier for a one-month trial. If the trial can provide the information needed to decide, the team has an option it had left off the list. The comparison changes as well. It now includes the cost of the trial, what the team could learn from it, and what options would remain at the end of the month.

**A detailed comparison can still miss anything left off the list.** This article uses public accounts from several decision-makers to illustrate seven ways to check a decision. Each move raises a question that is easy to overlook. Once we ask it, we can identify what it changes and decide how to continue the comparison.

## 1. Representing the problem and solving it within a framework

A **problem representation** sets out a decision before we make it. It specifies the options, the criteria for comparing them, the limits on what we can do, and the assumptions behind our judgement. Listing options and weighing their pros and cons is work within that representation. Discovering that a trial is possible, including exit costs in the comparison, or identifying an assumption that needs evidence can all change the representation itself.

A new angle is useful when we can say which part of the comparison it changes or what we need to check next. After changing the representation, we still need domain knowledge to judge whether the options are feasible and what they would cost.

## 2. Seven moves

### 2.1 Change the time horizon

Recalling his decision to leave Wall Street and start an online bookshop, Jeff Bezos said he imagined looking back at the age of 80. From that vantage point, giving up the immediate bonus mattered less, and missing the chance to try mattered more. [Academy of Achievement interview](https://achievement.org/achiever/jeffrey-p-bezos/)

This move asks **over what period we should weigh the gains and losses**. A longer horizon can reveal costs that seem small now and grow more important later. Then return to the present and check whether immediate limits on money, time, or responsibilities allow you to get that far.

### 2.2 Distinguish reversible and irreversible decisions

Bezos uses one-way and two-way doors to distinguish consequential commitments that are hard to undo from decisions that are easier to change. He argues that they call for different decision processes. [Amazon’s 2015 letter to shareholders](https://ir.aboutamazon.com/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF)

Make reversibility concrete. What state would we need to get back to? How long would it take, how much would it cost, and who would bear that cost? A decision can be reversible in name while money spent, time lost, or consequences for other people cannot be recovered. In the contract example, the team can try **changing how it commits**. A trial then becomes an option in its own right. How quickly to act depends on the consequences, the cost of reversing course, and the cost of waiting.

### 2.3 Look for conditions likely to remain stable over the horizon

When discussing retail strategy, Bezos identified low prices, fast delivery, and broad selection as things he expected customers to value over the long term. He used them to guide long-term investment. [Interview with Bezos](https://lexfridman.com/jeff-bezos-transcript)

The first move asks how a different time horizon changes the way we weigh gains and losses. This move asks **how stable the external conditions we rely on are likely to be over the chosen horizon**. Give priority to checking conditions that we have reason to expect will persist and that make a difference to whether the plan works. Record the horizon, the reasons for expecting stability, and what would invalidate that expectation.

### 2.4 Work backwards from failure

In his commencement address at the Harvard School, Charlie Munger drew on Jacobi’s idea of inversion. He described ways of inviting failure so that his listeners could see which paths they might avoid. [Munger’s 1986 address](https://worldlypartners.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/1986-commencement-speech-by-charlie-munger-at-harvard-school-now-harvard-westlake.pdf)

Suppose the plan has failed. Work backwards through the steps that could have led there. Ask separately **how failure could occur** and **whether we could recover from it**. If a risk could produce consequences we cannot bear, first identify what could block that path. Then decide whether the original plan is still feasible. After ruling out that path to failure, we still need to compare the options that remain.

### 2.5 Identify the limits of your judgement

Warren Buffett uses the circle of competence to explain that investors can confine their judgements to what they can evaluate. He stresses knowing where that boundary lies. [Berkshire Hathaway’s 1996 letter to shareholders](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)

This move asks **which parts of the decision I can judge reliably**. Can I explain what the outcome depends on, how far the evidence goes, and how I would spot an error? Pinpoint the gap before deciding whether to gather information, ask someone with experience to check, narrow the scope of the decision, or defer it.

### 2.6 Check whether the old relationship still holds

Andrew Grove calls the point at which a business’s fundamentals begin to change a strategic inflection point. He uses “10X change” in his book to draw attention to major shifts and discusses how to tell signal from noise. [Excerpt from *Only the Paranoid Survive*](https://penguinrandomhousehighereducation.com/book/?isbn=9780307574978)

Ask **whether a number has changed or the relationship the decision relied on has stopped holding**. If a supplier raises its price, we may only need to rerun the calculation. Suppose paying more used to secure expedited delivery, but the supplier has now shut down the production line. Continuing to assume that a higher bid will get us the goods on time leaves an infeasible option on the list. First write down what the old judgement would lead us to expect, then look for evidence that can distinguish the two explanations. The magnitude of a change can prompt a check. Whether to change the representation depends on what happened to the relevant condition or relationship.

### 2.7 Set review conditions before deciding

In another letter to shareholders, Bezos argued that recognising and correcting bad decisions quickly helps an organisation make decisions faster. [Amazon’s 2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)

Before deciding, write down **the basis for the judgement, when to review it, and what observation would reopen it**. If the purpose of a one-month trial is to check whether the supplier can deliver on time, agree beforehand on what results would support signing and what results would send the team back to look for another option. A review sometimes means updating a number. Sometimes it means checking whether the measure we chose answers the real question. This connects with [*Second-Order Reflection: Treating a Framework as a Phenotype*](./second-order-reflection.md). Checking a judgement and checking the standard used to make it are distinct tasks.

## 3. How the seven moves feed into a decision

All seven **check the current representation for omissions that could affect the decision**. Some change which gains and losses we weigh. Some change the options or the constraints on action. Others reveal gaps in evidence or competence, or establish how we will reconsider the decision as new information arrives.

Write down what changed. Did a new option appear, or did an existing one become infeasible? Did the criteria change, or did we identify an assumption to test first? If the new angle makes a difference to one of these, carry it into the next comparison. Choose whichever moves fit the decision at hand.

## 4. Limits

- **What the cases can show.** The public accounts illustrate the methods. Retrospective accounts from successful people are more likely to be recorded, while failures involving similar methods are less visible. Bezos also notes this bias in his 2015 discussion of one-way and two-way doors. The effect on outcomes requires a separate comparison.
- **Changing the representation can introduce errors.** Mistaking a reversible decision for an irreversible one can make the process needlessly heavy. Mistaking an ordinary fluctuation for an inflection point can lead us to discard a plan that still works. Check the evidence for the change and take account of the costs of checking and waiting.
- **Outcomes require a separate check.** Finding another option or noticing a risk sooner is a change in the process that we can record. Outcomes also depend on execution, resources, and external events.

## 5. Testable conjectures

1. **Reversibility.** A lighter process for decisions that are reversible at low cost may shorten decision time. Compare decisions with similar stakes and difficulty, recording serious errors and actual exit costs as well.
2. **Relatively stable conditions.** Relying on conditions judged in advance to be relatively stable and important to a plan may reduce later adjustments. Record the conditions, time horizon, and criteria for failure beforehand. Compare with similar plans that rely on short-term forecasts. Include plans whose assumptions about stability turned out to be wrong, and record errors of judgement separately from actual adjustment costs.
3. **Checking backwards from failure.** Identifying severe failure paths in advance and acting on them may reduce the corresponding extreme losses. Compare plans at similar levels of risk and record the paths identified, the measures taken, and the losses that occurred.
4. **Limits of judgement.** Writing down in advance which parts we can judge with confidence may lead to earlier requests for help or review where our judgement is uncertain. When comparing outcomes, also record which tasks people undertook, what risks they assumed, and which opportunities they deferred or passed up.
5. **Detecting inflection points.** Writing down what an established relationship would predict and checking it after repeated deviations may help us discover sooner that an existing plan no longer works. In comparable settings, measure the time from the first deviation to detection, and record false alarms as well.

## 6. A checklist before deciding

Start with whatever is most likely to change the decision.

1. **Time horizon.** What gains or losses become more important if I lengthen or shorten the horizon?
2. **Reversibility.** If I choose wrongly, what state could I return to, and what would it cost in time and resources? Can I commit in stages?
3. **Relatively stable conditions.** What does the plan rely on? What reason do I have to expect those conditions to hold over the period I care about?
4. **Failure paths.** Which route could lead to consequences I cannot bear? What could block it before I take it?
5. **Limits of judgement.** Which part can I assess? Which part do I need to check with other sources or people?
6. **Inflection points.** What evidence would distinguish ordinary variation from the failure of an established relationship?
7. **Review conditions.** When will I look again? What evidence would trigger a review?

Finish with one note. **What was I originally comparing, what specifically changed, and what do I still need to check?**
