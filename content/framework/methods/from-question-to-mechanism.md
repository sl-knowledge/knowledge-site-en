On 8 November 2026, the author and Claude discussed religion. The question moved from the credibility of a promise to religion's effects on individuals, then towards a mechanism draft involving practice, feedback and entry conditions. The main steps were as follows.

1. **From the truth of a promise to individual effects.** The author first asked how a promise about the future could be established, then asked how the system actually helps individuals. The discussion moved from broad prophecies to effects on personal life.

2. **Compare a book with religious participation.** The author noted that many books offer similar principles for living and asked why reading one might have different effects from participating in religion. Claude added implementation and reinforcement mechanisms involving authority, community, regular gatherings and identity. The comparison shifted from content to practice structures.

3. **Include participants in the feedback loop.** The author explicitly introduced reflexivity: participants place themselves within the system, their conduct and identification change, and they attribute changes in their lives to faith, reinforcing belief. Claude developed this into individual and group loops.

4. **From maintenance to initiation.** The author brought “why believe in the first place?” to the centre: before the benefits of practice have appeared, what initiates investment? Claude subsequently added entry routes involving family transmission, relationships preceding belief and life transitions.

5. **Use a walled-city metaphor to organise inside–outside differences.** The author used the metaphor to describe participants who may already have experienced effects, while nonparticipants may be blocked by a premise they have not accepted. The same loop was thus used to examine two different positions.

6. **From a single explanation to a generative mechanism.** Claude further suggested that belief may help sustain commitment, entry requirements differ among traditions, and social conditions affect participation. Together, these factors were organised into candidate variables and parameters. The author requested an abstract article, and Claude did the main drafting.

The main exchange, lasting twenty-odd minutes, illustrates how comparison, mechanism analysis and questions about initiation can organise a concrete problem into a potentially transferable model draft. The author kept adjusting the question to be explained; Claude supplied mechanisms, contrasting situations and help with expression. Previously developed ideas about constraints, feedback and Generators ran through the process.

## How reflexivity and the walled-city analogy entered the discussion

Reflexivity did not arise from nothing in this exchange. In the author's retrospective account, earlier financial research and discussions had already made the concept familiar. What was transferred here was the feedback relation in which understanding affects conduct, and the results of conduct affect understanding in turn.

The walled city supplied a different structure: a boundary distinguishes inside from outside; different positions give access to different experiences; and entry conditions affect whether internal experience becomes available. The author explicitly set aside the marriage context and did not copy the original metaphor's direction of entry and exit. Only selected relations were borrowed. The analogy itself neither establishes the causes of internal experiences nor guarantees that either side judges more accurately.

As to why this analogy came to mind at that moment, Claude offered a possible explanation: familiarity with the walled-city image in Chinese literature, together with recent discussions of marriage, may have made the associated memories more accessible. In retrospect, the author considered this plausible, although the connection had not been consciously apparent at the time. Claude also suggested that the process may have begun with recognising a relational structure and then finding a familiar vehicle for it. These explanations are compatible with the author's retrospective account, but the existing record cannot establish the actual sequence of association.

The abstraction therefore involved questions pursued in the moment, with earlier knowledge and recent discussions possibly supplying material. A process that can be articulated is to recognise relations in familiar material, specify which correspondences to retain and which content to leave aside, and then examine whether those relations help explain the current problem. Recording these clues makes the process more available for comparison and study than attributing the result solely to personal inspiration. It does not mean that the same association can necessarily be reproduced by following steps.

## Subsequent review and correction

The author then gave the conversation and draft to ChatGPT. ChatGPT summarised the development of the questions and the human–AI contributions, examining entry routes, outcome attribution and feedback direction. The author pointed out an error of level in this assessment: ChatGPT treated different entry routes as counterexamples to the framework without first checking whether they were manifestations of the same generative rule under different parameters. The original framework already distinguished entry types and gating strength. Review therefore needs to distinguish the generative rule, parameter conditions and particular manifestations before deciding whether a counterexample holds.
