## Overview

We often judge others by our own standards and rarely make those standards the subject of inquiry. When someone finds another way of saying something convoluted, it may genuinely be hard to follow. Or the listener may simply be used to organising language differently. To tell which is happening, we first need to say how we made the judgement.

I call the move from judging content to examining the framework of judgement **second-order reflection**. We can apply it to other people and turn it back on ourselves. Once we can see a framework, we can ask how it formed, when it serves us well, and what it tends to leave out. The article ends with testable conjectures and a checklist.

## 1. From judging content to seeing the framework

Here a **framework** is the set of distinctions a person draws, the information they consider, and the criteria they use to make a judgement. When deciding whether a sentence is clear, one person may focus on how concise it is. Another may care most about whether the listener can recount what happened in order.

| Where we direct our attention | A typical question |
|---|---|
| **First order.** Make a judgement within a framework. | Did the speaker make this clear? |
| **Second order.** Examine the framework of judgement. | How does this person decide whether the speaker made it clear? What does that criterion draw their attention to? |
| **Reflexive second order.** Put the same questions to ourselves. | On what grounds did I decide it was clear? What might my criterion have led me to overlook? |

There are two orders here. Reflexive second-order reflection is second-order reflection directed at oneself. Identifying that we use concision as a measure of clarity already brings part of our framework into view. How we acquired that habit is a further question.

## 2. Two examples of how speakers organise the same event

The focus here is on speakers and the standards they use to decide whether something has been said clearly. In both examples, we can start with the same question. What does the listener actually understand in this situation?

### Putting an event into a noun phrase or telling it in clauses

- *The committee’s rejection of the proposal surprised us.* The whole event, the committee rejecting the proposal, is packaged into a noun phrase and serves as the subject of the sentence.
- “<span lang="zh-CN">委员会否决了这个方案，我们没想到。</span>” One clause tells us the committee rejected the proposal. The next tells us we hadn’t expected it.

Both formulations give the rejection first and the reaction second. English can also use two clauses, as in *The committee rejected the proposal. We hadn’t expected that.* Mandarin can also use a noun phrase, as in “<span lang="zh-CN">委员会对这个方案的否决出乎我们的预料。</span>” Some people find the noun phrase compact. Others are more used to giving the event in one clause and the reaction in the next. The same person can switch between the two depending on the situation.

### Spelling out information or leaving it to the context

Suppose someone asks, “What did you do yesterday?” Here are two ways to answer.

- Mandarin. “<span lang="zh-CN">昨天看了两部电影。</span>”
- English. *I watched two films yesterday.*

In the Mandarin reply, the question makes it clear that the person answering is talking about themselves, so <span lang="zh-CN">我</span> (“I”) can be left out. <span lang="zh-CN">昨天</span> (“yesterday”) tells us when it happened, and <span lang="zh-CN">了</span> marks the watching as an event that took place. <span lang="zh-CN">两部</span> gives the number, and <span lang="zh-CN">电影</span> (“film”) keeps the same form. The full English sentence spells out the subject, *I*. It also uses the past tense *watched* and the plural *films*, even though *yesterday* and *two* already tell us the time and number.

Both languages can draw on context. English speakers also leave subjects out in casual conversation. Mandarin speakers can state the subject when they need to identify or emphasise who did something. The comparison here is about how speakers, in the same situation, divide the work between the sentence and its context. Section 9 points to research on time, number, and subjects.

Someone familiar with the Mandarin reply might think, “You just asked me. Do I really need to say ‘I’? If I’ve said ‘yesterday’ and ‘two’, why change ‘watch’ and ‘film’ as well?” Someone accustomed to full English sentences might feel that a sentence is only complete once it says who watched the films. Each person may be judging by a standard that feels familiar to them.

**My hypothesis is that when two ways of saying something both get the same event across, people may find the one they know better more natural and, for that reason, tend to judge it clearer.**

We can ask two questions separately. What does the listener actually understand from the sentence and its context? Which formulation feels more natural to me, and what grounds do I have for calling it clearer?

| What a speaker does | What they see |
|---|---|
| Judges another way of speaking by the one they know best | The sentence seems unnecessarily complicated, or the other person seems to leave out something that ought to be said. |
| Switches between two ways of speaking | The same event can be organised in different ways, with some information left to the context where appropriate. |
| Notices the standard the other person uses | They can identify whether the other person values concision, following the events in order, or spelling out the key information in the sentence. |
| Puts the same questions to themselves | They can distinguish what feels familiar to them from what the listener actually understands. |

Someone who can switch between two ways of speaking may still treat the more familiar one as the default. Encountering a different way gives us a chance to notice our own habit. We can still prefer one way after noticing that.

## 3. Treating the framework as a phenotype

Once we see a framework, we can treat it as the outcome of processes operating under particular conditions. That is what I mean here by a **phenotype**. Experience, environment, and needs offer leads for an explanation. Comparing how the same person writes reports and tells stories about similar events can show what expression they choose in different settings. To go further and suggest that years of writing reports produced a particular habit, we would need to look back at when their expression began changing and what else changed at the time.

To understand before evaluating, ask three questions.

1. **How did the framework form?** What experiences or conditions helped produce it?
2. **Why do people accept it?** What problem does it solve for them? Why does it make sense by their own standards?
3. **Do its claims stand up to scrutiny?** What evidence can be checked independently? What does this framework’s standard leave out, and who bears the costs?

How a framework formed and why people accept it require their own evidence. A claim still stands or falls on the evidence that supports it. Our own criteria for evaluating it also arose under particular conditions and can be examined in the same way.

## 4. Understanding and choosing

Once we understand how a framework formed, we still need to say which criteria we will use to choose. When a friend has just asked what you did yesterday, “Watched two films” can be enough. If the sentence will stand on its own in a record that someone may use later to find out who watched the films, it is worth naming the person. How much to spell out depends on what the listener already knows and how the sentence will be used.

> **The order at which we think does not determine what we choose, but it affects how we hold that choice.**

Reflection gives us an extra opportunity to examine a choice. We can continue to stand by it while making clear what it serves, when it applies, and what would lead us to reconsider.

## 5. Why we need to turn the examination back on ourselves

It is hard to see the whole framework while we are using it. Familiar standards often come into play without a second thought. With little to compare them against, they may seem obvious. When an issue touches our identity or a long-standing commitment, it may be harder to stop and examine them.

After becoming aware of part of a framework, we may fall back on familiar ways of judging in another setting. Reflexive second-order reflection is something we have to return to. We can look for three observable signs that a person has returned to it. Can they **identify the standard they used, name something they may have overlooked, and say what evidence would lead them to revise the standard**? Once these conditions are met, it makes sense to record how long it took. We can also see whether the person can carry out the same examination with a new example.

## 6. How to examine our own framework

Suppose I listen to an explanation and think the speaker left out a crucial step. On checking it sentence by sentence, I find that the step was there. I revise that particular judgement. I can then go further and notice that I often take “concise” as evidence that an explanation is clear. The criterion itself is now what I want to test.

I can prepare two passages that convey the same key steps and ask readers to recount them. If I thought the more concise version was clearer and the readers reconstruct the steps more accurately from the longer version, I have reason to assess concision and clarity separately for this task. How much each matters depends on the task. I am now examining how I have been scoring explanations and when that way of scoring is useful.

- **Write down the criteria in advance.** State separately what observation would overturn this particular judgement and what observation would make you revise the criterion you used.
- **Ask someone else what you missed.** When someone reaches a different conclusion, ask what they based it on and what they considered that you overlooked.
- **Try a new example.** See whether the revised criterion accounts for the difference in the original example and whether it works on another. Act on the current judgement where appropriate, and record what would trigger a fresh examination.

## 7. Testable conjectures

1. People who can state their own criteria and their limits may be quicker, when faced with an unfamiliar view, to give an account that the person holding it recognises. Assess the quality of the account on a different case and record the speed of understanding separately from attitudes such as hostility.
2. When a question concerns their identity or a long-standing commitment, the same person may be more likely to rely on familiar criteria. Compare questions of similar difficulty and observe whether they can meet the conditions in Section 5.
3. People who write down what would call their criteria into question may be more likely to revisit them when that evidence appears. Record separately whether they examined the criterion and whether they had grounds for the revision they made.
4. Repeated practice in first restating someone else’s framework and then examining one’s own comparative criteria may make it easier to do both in a new case. Test this with materials people have not practised on.

## 8. Checklist

1. Am I checking one judgement or examining the framework I used to make it?
2. What standard is the other person using? Can I give an account of their reasons that they recognise, then check the evidence and costs?
3. What conditions could have shaped their framework? If my explanation is right, what should change when those conditions change?
4. What standard did I use? Even if I have adopted a more elaborate theory, do I still treat a familiar standard as the only one?
5. What might this standard leave out? What evidence would make me revise it? Can I try it on a new case?
6. Having understood the framework, on what grounds will I choose? When should I re-examine that standard?
