*A record of how questions developed in human–AI collaboration · Case record v1.0.2 · 9 November 2026*

## Starting with an apparently ordinary ethical dispute

This research did not begin with a plan to build a framework for the structure and generative mechanisms of disagreement in ethics.

The starting point was quite ordinary: once we understand that someone's behaviour is shaped by their history, knowledge, circumstances and real constraints, how should we regard their responsibility? Does compassion for someone conflict with preventing them from harming others?

The AI initially answered along familiar ethical lines: distinguish explaining behaviour, evaluating it and deciding what to do. Understanding someone's circumstances does not mean endorsing their conduct; compassion for someone causing harm need not prevent protecting those harmed.

This was a reasonable answer. But rather than continuing to ask how to reconcile compassion and justice, the researcher changed the question:

> “We actually need to step outside the traditional linguistic framework of philosophy and discuss this at a higher, more abstract level.”

That statement began a series of shifts. What is worth recording is not an especially impressive AI answer, but the researcher's decision, after receiving a usable answer, to ask again: **what problem should we actually be researching?**

## First shift: study the disagreement before joining it

The AI first tried treating ethics as a problem of coordination among multiple agents: people have different goals, resources are limited and actions affect one another, creating a need for rules. The researcher then adjusted the direction again:

> “We should not start by looking for a direction of our own. The key is first to study … the ethical research that already exists.”

In other words, do not begin by inventing a new ethical principle. Philosophers have debated these issues for a long time. Treat those existing arguments as research material and ask a different question:

**Why do they reach different answers, and why do they remain unable to persuade one another?**

The research target changed from choosing an answer to explaining how multiple answers arise.

The AI helped by organising consequentialism, deontology, value conflict and existing work on moral disagreement into objects of comparison. But the researcher insisted that material must not be selected merely to support the new framework: the actual arguments of earlier thinkers should be used to test it.

## Second shift: five people and one person—why is the question framed this way?

The discussion reached a classic thought experiment: five patients need organs; may one healthy person be sacrificed to save them?

The AI initially offered the familiar explanation: some theories emphasise outcomes, while others emphasise limits on permissible action.

The researcher first noticed missing conditions: who are the five patients and the healthy person? Would changing their ages, circumstances or identities change the judgement?

This was not a new moral answer but a modelling requirement: **do not silently treat unspecified parameters as settled.**

The AI distinguished two issues: factual parameters may affect the result, while whether a parameter *ought* to affect an ethical judgement concerns the evaluation rule. It also noted that classic thought experiments sometimes deliberately fix conditions to isolate a principle. This differs from omitting information in a real decision.

The discussion could have continued by adding more parameters. But the researcher soon changed the question again.

## Third shift: the issue is not only missing parameters; the representation may not be unique

The researcher said:

> “Its representation itself is not just one.”

This was one of the exchange's central turns.

Adding ages, health conditions and resources to a fixed model assumes we already know which model to use. Ethical disagreement may go further: **different theories need not be solving the same formal problem.**

One theory seeks to maximise aggregate outcomes; another first excludes impermissible actions; another recognises several values that cannot simply be put in one order.

Disagreement may therefore concern more than different numerical inputs: what is the target? What is a hard constraint? Who is included? What counts as an acceptable answer?

The AI expanded this intuition into mathematical and logical forms, including single-objective optimisation, constrained choice, multi-objective evaluation and incomplete orderings.

Those forms did not establish which ethical theory was correct. They helped show that **people claiming to answer the same question may be using different problem structures.**

## Fourth shift: who is asking, and who is answering?

The researcher then identified a premise that had repeatedly been overlooked:

> “Every question has a questioner and an answerer.”

The same organ-transplant event can become different questions for a patient, the healthy person, a doctor, a legislator and a philosopher.

The patient asks how to obtain treatment; the healthy person asks whether others have a right to sacrifice them; the doctor asks what actions are permitted; the legislator asks what rules society should establish; the philosopher asks whether a principle holds.

The AI organised this reminder into four roles:

| Role | Question to make explicit |
| --- | --- |
| Q — Questioner | Who turned the situation into this question? |
| J — Judge | Who gives the answer, using what reasons? |
| A — Actor | Who actually has the capability and authority to act? |
| S — Stakeholders | Who bears the consequences, and who might have been omitted? |

The AI formulated the Q/J/A/S checklist; the researcher's central shift was that **the questioner must not remain hidden outside the question.**

This does not give a questioner the authority to declare an answer correct. It makes it easier to identify whose viewpoint is expressed, whose interests are counted, who may act and who merely bears the consequences.

## Fifth shift: why can ethics not be separated from human generators?

The researcher connected the discussion to a longstanding concern: modelling human behaviour.

The point was not to ask the AI immediately to measure everyone's psychological parameters. Ethical questions, standards of evaluation, institutional rules and arguments about justification are proposed, discussed and enacted by people. Different periods and groups have different histories, interests, environments and conditions of understanding, and may develop different ethical views.

The researcher borrowed an economic perspective: limited resources, different goals, mutual effects and conflicts of interest do not disappear when we use the word “ethics”.

The AI developed feedback relations among individual judgements, group interactions and institutions. It also noted a boundary: scarcity does not make every interaction zero-sum. Cooperation, technology and institutions may expand the feasible space.

Ethics could thus be examined as more than a debate among static principles in books. **Disputes themselves may take new forms as social environments, capabilities for action, interests and institutions change.**

## Sixth shift: identifying a difficult problem does not make solving it mandatory

As the AI continued into moral-psychology experiments and the identifiability of judgement mechanisms, the researcher returned the discussion to its target:

> “Would it be enough just to point out this difficulty? … Must we necessarily develop a method for identifying all these different … generators?”

This exposed a common drift in human–AI collaboration: on finding a worthwhile side question, an AI may keep expanding it until it becomes the main task.

The researcher did not reject psychological experiments. The requirement was to separate two questions:

- **The structural why:** given two ethical arguments, why do they reach different answers?
- **The historical and psychological why:** why does a particular person accept one of those theories?

The first was the current task; the second could be left for later work or other researchers.

**Explaining where arguments diverge does not require first knowing the philosophers' childhoods, personalities or brain mechanisms.**

This gave the research a clear boundary.

## Seventh shift: converge on a map of argumentative disagreement

The researcher then clarified the work's value:

> “Help people really understand the problem … understand the different sources, the sources of arguments, and where their disagreement with one another comes from.”

The AI proposed three candidate diagnostic dimensions:

- **H1, differences in agent position:** from what positions, goals and interests do the parties proceed?
- **H2, differences in representation:** what does each treat as a target, constraint or decisive reason?
- **H3, structural incompatibility:** under the given conditions, can the requirements maintained by the parties be satisfied together?

The AI proposed these three dimensions; the researcher accepted them and asked that they be applied to existing debates, rather than turning to the identification of every individual's psychological generator.

They were not a universal explanation. A useful result would be a map both sides could examine: which facts do they share? At which premise do they diverge? Which changes might bring them closer? Which differences remain?

**Establishing that two values cannot simply be combined can itself clarify a disagreement.**

## Eighth shift: from present disputes to a method that may remain useful

The researcher further noted that ethical questions will not remain a fixed collection. As periods, technologies and interest groups change, new questions will arise.

The AI proposed a future-AI ethics example: if highly autonomous AI emerges, people may dispute whether it is a tool, an agent or potentially a subject of moral concern; who has authority to control it; and whether safety, autonomy and rights conflict.

Agent roles, representation and constraints could again be examined.

The researcher decided to include the example without demanding immediate proof that the framework could predict every future ethical dispute. This retained an important discipline: **fitting a problem into a framework does not establish that the framework explains it.**

Another AI subsequently offered an external review: explaining long-standing ethical disagreement requires serious comparison with earlier work.

The most important comparison was Rawls's burdens of judgement: conscientious reasoning can still lead to disagreement because of difficulties involving evidence, concepts, weights, experience and values.

This cautioned against renaming established ideas and claiming originality.

The review also proposed two useful revisions: identify facts and concepts separately as **H0**, and test whether an H3 claim of no solution results from an unduly narrow set of options.

After comparing and revising these suggestions, the AI incorporated them into later versions and used Sen's three children and a flute as a comparison: one child can play it, one most lacks toys, and one made it. These facts can all hold, and the three fairness arguments can all merit consideration. The main disagreement concerns which principle should govern allocation rather than the truth of those facts.

The document had now developed into v0.4, with a research target, a diagnostic framework, connections to earlier work and a case that could be checked step by step.

## Looking back: what did the human and the AI contribute?

The process is worth recording neither because the AI was always correct nor because the researcher single-handedly declared a completed theory.

**The researcher mainly contributed four things.**

First, repeatedly checking whether the problem was well posed. Whenever the discussion moved too far along a familiar path, the researcher asked why the question had to be asked that way.

Second, connecting disciplines: ethical conflict was related to human generators, economic scarcity, multiple agents, constraints and feasibility.

Third, controlling the research target: useful analytical tools were accepted, while identifying everyone's ethical generator was explicitly excluded as a mandatory task.

Fourth, returning to existing research: the framework was to explain actual debates, rather than being declared correct before examples were sought to confirm it.

**The AI mainly provided four other kinds of help.**

It organised emerging ideas into comparable structures; supplied classic cases and prior philosophical work; expressed intuitions through role tables, condition tests and set constraints; and revised the target after correction, producing successive drafts.

The AI's initial normative answer was a reasonable response to the question about responsibility and compassion at that time. As the target shifted to analysing argumentative disagreement, the response needed to shift too. Once that target was clear, continuing to make parameter identification and psychological experiments the central task constituted drift.

The collaboration was a process of **iterative correction**: the researcher adjusted the question, the AI expanded and examined it, and that expansion exposed further boundaries needing clarification.

## What distinguishes this path of questioning?

The final v0.4 contains H0–H3, Q/J/A/S, feasible sets and a map of argumentative disagreement. The conversation shows that these tools were not designed all at once.

| Question being considered | The question the researcher moved towards |
| --- | --- |
| Do compassion and responsibility conflict? | Why use the traditional way of posing the ethical question? |
| Which ethical principle is more reasonable? | Where do existing ethical schools actually diverge? |
| How should the parameters of the five-versus-one case be completed? | Might the ethical question lack a unique representation? |
| Which ethical conclusion is correct? | Who asks, answers, acts and bears the consequences? |
| How can each person's ethical generator be identified? | Does the current research need to solve individual psychological identification? |
| Can the framework explain existing cases? | Can it analyse ethical questions that emerge in the future? |
| Is this a new theory? | What does it add to the work of Rawls, Sen and others? |

The common feature is that **the researcher did not merely request a more detailed answer, but repeatedly changed the question the answer was meant to serve.**

The AI helped make each revised question more explicit and supplied material against which it could be checked.

## What has been obtained, and what remains open?

The result is a usable, revisable research outline intended to identify where ethical arguments diverge, without rushing to decide for either side.

The conversation cannot establish that the framework explains more than Rawls, Sen or existing methods. Organising a few cases does not establish that H0–H3 exhaust all disagreements.

The open test is whether, when faced with an unfamiliar ethical dispute, the framework can identify the key points of disagreement faster and more accurately than existing methods. Can it predict which changed premises would alter a conclusion? Can it acknowledge and respond to cases it cannot explain?

Those questions remain open. As a human–AI research case, the exchange also illustrates something else:

> **Sometimes progress comes from recognising that the original question needs to be posed again, rather than merely obtaining a better answer.**
>
> **The human proposes a new perspective; the AI helps expand, compare, organise and examine it; the human then checks whether this remains a worthwhile research question.**

This is not a mandatory division of labour for humans or AI. It is one collaborative path observable in this particular exchange.
