> **Public edition v0.1 · values and research governance.** Organized from the research note of the same title, retaining two ideas: objectives should not continually destroy their sustaining conditions, and future choice and error correction deserve protection. It adds distinctions between facts and values, the standing of affected people, and procedures for setting and revising goals. These are contestable normative positions, not a uniquely derived social optimum from physical laws.

## 1. Why must an objective function itself be explained?

Complex-system research asks how to improve efficiency, maintain stability, accelerate knowledge or expand future options. Such questions already assume what is worth pursuing. Without discussing objectives, powerful prediction and optimization may simply move a system more effectively in a questionable direction.

“Objective function” is used broadly for the purposes and evaluations through which actions and outcomes are compared. It does not require every value to fit a numerical formula. Actual problems may involve multiple purposes, hard limits and unresolved disagreement.

At least three forms of justification must be distinguished:

| Level | Question | What remains unanswered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feasibility | Can it be achieved with available resources, time and conditions? | Is the achievable worth doing? |
| Instrumental rationality | Given the goal, does the action help attain it? | Is the goal legitimate? |
| Justification of goals and procedures | Why pursue it, who is affected and who may decide? | Efficiency or existing power alone cannot answer |

A feasible and efficient arrangement may be unjust. A worthwhile purpose may require different means or conditions. Justifying an objective connects these questions.

## 2. Survival conditions constrain action but do not choose our values

People have bodies, recovery needs, dependencies and limited time. Sustained social activity also depends on resources, ecology and cooperation. A plan exhausting its own operating conditions may fail to achieve its promised long-term goals.

This is a sustainability check. A long-term learning arrangement that consumes all rest and living resources must explain how it can continue. Observation and evidence can test that question.

But “this system can persist” and “this system deserves preservation” are distinct judgments. An arrangement harming some people can remain stable for a time. Stability is not moral proof; respect for those affected should not depend solely on whether oppression reduces efficiency.

Moving from fact to obligation requires additional value premises. [Hume discusses such inferential transitions near the end of *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book III, Part I, Section I](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/3/full). The reference demands explicit grounds for our argument, not a resolution of all metaethics.

This article explicitly accepts a normative starting point: **affected people should be taken seriously as subjects with lives and purposes of their own; their basic dignity and participation cannot be measured solely by their output toward others’ goals.** Survival facts constrain implementation; this premise explains why human circumstances matter.

## 3. Optimization for whom? “The whole” must not conceal different people

Objectives depend on the object of inquiry and who enters the evaluation. Managers, investors, developers, users and other affected groups may face different benefits and costs.

Describing an actor’s goal does not endorse it. A goal serving a decision maker’s interests must still explain its effects on others and why they should accept it.

“For society as a whole” also needs unpacking: who benefits, who pays, and are burdens concentrated on those with little voice? Who weights present and future generations? Which current burdens can uncertain future benefits justify, on what evidence and within what limits?

Society should not be treated as a person with one will, nor should collective persistence automatically outrank each individual life. Justification includes handling conflicts, allocating costs and protecting dissent.

The original note discusses intergenerational continuity. Here that means sustaining conditions for future lives and supporting autonomous family and reproductive choices. A need for social continuity does not imply an individual duty to reproduce.

## 4. Indicators inform judgment but must not silently replace purposes

Output, technical performance, stability, satisfaction and option counts can be informative. Problems arise when they become the whole purpose and relevant differences disappear.

The original uses GDP, technology, stability, equality, freedom and happiness as examples. The public edition limits these to warnings: ignoring conditions, costs and distribution can produce outcomes conflicting with the purpose pursued. It does not claim every single objective inevitably causes disaster, or enough indicators automatically avoid problems.

| Apparent improvement | Further questions |
| --- | --- |
| Higher total output | How do costs, ecology, time burdens and benefit distribution change? |
| Better technical performance | Who can use it, who bears transition costs, and does it support human judgment? |
| More stable apparent order | Can dissent be expressed, errors discovered and necessary changes made? |
| More listed options | Can people actually enter, exit and bear the consequences? |
| Higher average satisfaction | Whose circumstances are hidden by averaging, and is the evaluation reliable? |

These questions expose blind spots without determining final weights. Value conflicts require explicit discussion rather than concealment in a supposedly neutral score.

## 5. Long-term openness: a defensible direction, not a universal maximization theorem

I retain the original direction of “long-term effective options”: **subject to respect for basic human standing and necessary living and ecological conditions, value people’s real future ability to learn, act, adjust and choose again.**

The rationale is limited knowledge and an uncertain future. Today’s decision makers may not know the best future form of life. Preserving correction and rechoice reduces the risk of one judgment permanently fixing everyone’s path.

“Effective” matters. A path without resources, time, information or access is not a meaningful opportunity merely because it appears on a list. Compare [Amartya Sen’s discussion of basic capabilities in “Equality of What?”](https://ophi.org.uk/sites/default/files/Sen-1979_Equality-of-What.pdf). The present framework must still defend itself; a related concept does not supply a ready-made conclusion.

Openness also costs something. Learning requires investment, relationships require commitment, and important work may demand sustained focus. Refusing commitment to keep every option open can damage life and capability.

The article therefore neither maximizes option counts nor makes future openness the sole value. Consider it alongside present life, equal standing, responsibility and justified commitment. [Freedom and Ease](./freedom-and-future-options.md) explores the distinction: knowing other paths exist does not require continually changing the one being followed.

## 6. Why dignity cannot be only “a variable useful to the system”

Experience and judgment affect cooperation and action—an empirical question worth studying. But if dignity matters only because it improves cooperation, people can still be excluded when temporarily “unproductive.”

The normative position goes further: recognition should not depend only on output. Even a disrespectful practice that looks effective in a model faces objections about standing, choice and justification.

[Section II of Kant’s *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm), treating humanity as an end and not merely a means, is a relevant philosophical reference. It does not directly yield a full institutional program here, but supports rejecting people as mere inputs to growth, stability or technology.

This does not forbid public arrangements requiring contributions or burdens. It requires reasons, allocation, authorization and limits, with real channels for expression, challenge and correction. Explaining costs is more responsible than erasing them through grand goals.

## 7. Who chooses, by what standard, and is feedback genuine?

Goal justification returns to procedures for formulation and revision. Expertise helps compare consequences but does not itself authorize deciding everyone’s purposes. A consultation does not automatically establish adequate consideration of all circumstances.

Continue asking: Who proposes goals and sets weights? Who can object? Who bears errors and holds verification material? Does negative feedback enter decisions, or do records retain only support for the original plan?

Genuine feedback has at least two dimensions. Factually, it needs sources, measurement definitions and independent checks that can reveal disappointing effects. Procedurally, affected people must be able to express losses omitted by original indicators and challenge the evaluation itself.

Empirical results can show whether promises were met and costs omitted; they cannot alone settle every value conflict. Even achieved goals can be reasonably opposed, as can distributions.

Goal-setting cannot end with “trust the designers.” It requires intelligible reasons, participation, conditions for dissent, and arrangements for revision, termination and accountability. These are the article’s procedural commitments; their concrete form needs further design for particular problems and participants.

## 8. Record values so they can be challenged and revised

A useful objective statement need not begin as an optimization formula:

| Item | What must be explained |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | What should improve, and why is it worthwhile? |
| Affected parties | Who benefits, who pays and who may be omitted? |
| Value premises | Which principles are accepted, and where does disagreement remain? |
| Actual conditions | What resources, time, ecological and implementation conditions apply? |
| Evaluation and evidence | What do indicators measure or miss, and how is this checked? |
| Alternatives | Why choose this arrangement, and what costs do alternatives carry? |
| Updating and responsibility | Which outcomes require revision, stopping or reopening goals, and who handles this? |

**Fictional example: an adult-learning program.** “Increase course completions” may satisfy reporting without showing whether participants use learning in their lives. “Support actual capabilities participants have reason to value” requires asking what they want to improve, comparing time and fee burdens, checking application, and retaining exit and adjustment options.

The second formulation does not guarantee success. Organizers can still prescribe what participants should value or reproduce old problems with capability scores. Participation and feedback need concrete arrangements; program effects and value disagreements must be reported separately.

The original says goals should not destroy the subjects realizing them. The public edition retains this as a consistency check on long-term goals, adding a more important requirement: even a self-sustaining goal must explain why it is worthwhile, who decides and how it treats those carrying it.

Basic standing, actual capabilities and future correction opportunities are this article’s explicit value starting points. They require public defense. After accepting them, evidence constrains means and tests promises, while affected people participate in revision. That is the justification proposed here.
