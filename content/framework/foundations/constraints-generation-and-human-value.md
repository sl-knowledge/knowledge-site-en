## Abstract

This outline connects three questions without treating them as interchangeable: how the world and human behaviour take shape; what warrants confidence in an explanation; and, given explicit value premises, how we should treat one another. Its condensation is a relational structure connecting generative explanation, evidential discipline and multi-agent ethical judgement, together with a six-part ethical check. It does not derive moral obligations directly from causal regularities or equate understanding another person with removing responsibility. Its value commitments are candidate principles requiring independent defence. The outline identifies connections and questions for examination rather than claiming a completed, closed philosophical system.

## Knowledge condensation: three levels, three kinds of argument

| Level | Connections made here | Checks that remain necessary |
|---|---|---|
| Description and knowledge | Conditions, generative mechanisms, representation and evidence updating | Is the explanation supported? Are there rival explanations? |
| Value premises | Equal concern, basic well-being, real options and responsibility | Why accept them, and how are conflicts justified? |
| Practical judgement | Multi-agent effects, feasible arrangements, repair and feedback | Who bears costs? Is the arrangement feasible? Do outcomes require revision? |

Description helps identify the conditions facing a value judgement, but cannot alone prove a value. Section 2 supplies a minimal check; section 3 sets out relations requiring argument; later sections develop their grounds.

This outline differs in role from [The Generative Mechanisms of Ethical Judgement and Social Norms](https://sl-knowledge.github.io/knowledge-site-en/framework/methods/ethical-disagreement-map). That article organises the sources and locations of ethical disagreement. This one proposes candidate value commitments and asks how they can be justified. Explaining disagreement does not establish that a choice is right.

## 1. What does building a philosophical system involve?

A philosophical system is more than an appealing central idea or a collection of similar sayings. It needs compatible answers to several connected questions:

| Level | Question | Current direction |
|---|---|---|
| Worldview and ontology | What relations connect the world and human beings? | Generation, conditions, environment, constraints and change |
| Epistemology and methodology | What makes an explanation credible? | Representation first, evidence updating, disconfirmation and boundaries |
| Philosophy of human beings | How do character, beliefs, actions and choices develop? | Distinct histories, individual parameters and real feasible spaces |
| Value theory | What deserves care, and why? | Life, well-being, dignity, goodwill and real opportunities for choice |
| Ethics | What is justified when actions affect others? | Equal concern, avoiding harm, fairness, responsibility and repair |
| Practical philosophy | How should understanding and judgement affect action? | Understanding without permissiveness; compassion without imposing it; protection and improvement |

Not every philosopher must cover all six areas. Specialised work on knowledge, language or scientific method may be mature. **But if the aim is a broader worldview addressing the world, life and how people should live together, ethics cannot be an optional appendix.** Explanation alone cannot establish why victims should be protected or responsibility taken.

**Structural completeness differs from sound argument.** Adding an ethics chapter does not make a theory mature; a carefully structured account still needs counterexamples, conceptual clarification and empirical examination.

## 2. From judgement to action: a minimal ethical check

In a practical dispute, before assigning labels of good or evil, check:

1. **Facts and generative conditions:** what is known and unknown? How did harm arise?
2. **All affected parties:** who has interests, rights and risks? Have silent or disadvantaged parties been omitted?
3. **Values and boundaries:** which basic protections, rights and reasonable expectations cannot be sacrificed arbitrarily?
4. **Feasible arrangements:** what can actually be done? Can victims be protected while unnecessary harm is reduced?
5. **Responsibility and consequences:** how can prevention, repair, fairness and long-term protection be combined with procedural safeguards for those held responsible?
6. **Feedback and revision:** did outcomes improve? Does new evidence require changing the original judgement?

This is a protocol for reflection, not an automatic verdict replacing law, evidential procedures or complex ethical argument.

## 3. Result structure: possible compatibility and remaining arguments

| Apparently conflicting claims | A possible distinction |
|---|---|
| Behaviour has generative conditions; people are responsible | Causation explains emergence; responsibility also requires examining capability, control, intention and fair procedure |
| Understand and feel compassion for a person; oppose their action | Understanding formation does not endorse harm |
| Respect diverse lives; retain ethical boundaries | Plural paths do not justify arbitrary deprivation of others' basic rights |
| Protect freedom; sometimes restrict it | Proportionate restrictions on ongoing harm may be necessary to protect others' basic freedoms |
| Understand causes; experience different emotions | Observers have their own conditions; ethical duties cannot simply be identified with an emotion |
| Study feasible space; do not make freedom the sole value | Feasible space matters, without replacing rights and fairness with one number |

These distinctions offer a possible compatibility account, not a proof of the whole outline's consistency. Even demonstrated compatibility would not adequately defend the value premises or establish real-world effects.

The main open issue is not whether to acknowledge individual differences—that is already a starting point—but how to justify values and make defensible choices when life, rights, fairness, freedom and aggregate well-being conflict.

## 4. Worldview: nobody exists independently of conditions

The starting perspective is that internal mechanisms, historical states, external environments and real constraints jointly influence states and change. This also applies to human beings.

Cognition, belief, emotion and action emerge through interactions among experience, learning, bodily conditions, social relationships, opportunities and circumstances. Different starting conditions and developmental paths can produce different patterns.

Two boundaries matter:

**Being influenced by conditions does not imply inability to act.** Thought, choice, learning, organisation and self-revision are themselves parts of causal processes and can alter future feasible space. The account need not assume an unproved strict fatalism.

**“Different people have different parameters” is a starting point, not an all-purpose explanation.** It cautions against expecting identical responses. Explaining a specific difference still requires identifying conditions, directions of effect and evidence; otherwise “different parameters” is unfalsifiable shorthand.

## 5. Epistemology: reconstruct the question before updating evidence

If people and societies are complex dynamic systems, understanding them cannot start by choosing a familiar label and fitting everything into it.

The research discipline here is:

1. **Representation first:** specify the object, target and boundary, and ask whether the question is framed appropriately.
2. **Generative mechanism (Generator):** work from outcomes towards processes, examining interactions among states, environment, constraints, actions and feedback.
3. **CF Grammar:** where appropriate, distinguish System, Target, Environment, Horizon, State, Constraint, Buffer and Binding, rather than filling missing information through intuition.
4. **Evidence updating:** separate observations, explanations, value judgements and conjectures; seek evidence that could change the original assessment.
5. **The observer is also in the system:** knowledge, attention, emotions and values have histories. Explaining others does not automatically make one's own explanation correct.

These are mainly principles for **knowing**, not ethical conclusions. They may help explain how harm occurs without alone establishing why it should be prevented.

## 6. Understanding limitations without erasing effort

A generative perspective cautions against simply comparing lives. An apparently obvious choice may never have entered another person's recognised or practically feasible space.

Placing behaviour within history and circumstances may reduce hasty personal blame.

Understanding limitations need not deny agency. Under limited conditions, people still learn, try, adjust and attempt to live well; some help others despite their own difficulties. Such efforts deserve recognition.

**Whether understanding produces compassion, calm, respect or another emotion also depends on the observer's conditions.** Causal understanding has no universal emotional endpoint. Understanding, emotional response and normative duty must be considered separately; no particular feeling is a compulsory stage of understanding a mechanism.

**Being easily moved and being obliged to respect ethical principles are different questions.** Even without strong empathy, reasons and institutions can support respect for life, freedom and rights.

## 7. A crucial boundary: facts do not automatically yield an “ought”

Norms may be explained through cooperation, trust, conflict and power. It does not follow that every rule sustaining order is justified: unjust institutions can also persist.

Explaining why someone commits violence is descriptive; judging that violence should be restricted is ethical. The former does not automatically entail the latter.

This is the is–ought transition problem. Hume cautioned against moving without explanation from descriptive propositions to normative ones. See [Hume's Moral Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral/#IsOugh).

Value premises must therefore be explicit, rather than presented as necessary consequences of natural laws.

## 8. Value theory: what do we choose to value?

These are **preliminary value commitments** proposed for further defence, not conclusions already proved by natural regularities:

- **Life and basic well-being deserve concern.** Assessment must include whether all affected parties can live safely, not only whether an agent reaches a target.
- **People have equal basic ethical standing.** Differences in origin, capability, status or belief do not justify arbitrary deprivation of basic interests.
- **Real feasible space deserves protection.** Freedom concerns the actual conditions for action and development, beyond paper choices.
- **Goodwill, learning and effort within limited circumstances deserve recognition.** Effort does not automatically make conduct right or replace assessment of consequences.
- **Lives may follow diverse paths.** Without infringement of others' basic rights, there is no need to force others to accept one's own faith, theory or way of life.

“Feasible space” invites comparison with the **capability approach** developed by Amartya Sen and others: attention to what people can actually do and be, rather than resources alone. See [The Capability Approach](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/). Similarity does not establish equivalence or substitute that approach for this outline's argument.

The values are not reduced to one score. More options for one person do not justify depriving others of basic safety; greater aggregate benefit does not permit arbitrary sacrifice of minority rights. Real tensions may remain.

## 9. Causal understanding and ethical judgement can coexist

### 9.1 Three questions to keep separate

When conduct harms others, ask:

**Causal:** why did it happen? Which experiences, beliefs, incentives and environments contributed?

**Ethical:** did it violate rights or cause unjustified harm? What legitimate claims do affected parties have?

**Practical:** how should victims now be protected, continuing danger restricted and responsibility established, while losses are repaired and recurrence prevented where possible?

All three can be answered together without contradiction.

### 9.2 A provisional ethical principle

> Given equal basic ethical standing, respect justified autonomous choices and avoid unnecessary harm, deception and coercion. When interests conflict, prioritise basic safety and rights, address conflict fairly and proportionately in ways open to scrutiny, and preserve real long-term possibilities for all parties where possible.

This is a **candidate normative principle**, not a mathematical theorem derived from Generator. It still needs answers concerning the boundaries of rights, the meaning of fairness and conflict under scarcity.

### 9.3 How to regard a person who acts wrongly

People need not be classified as inherently bad, nor should knowledge of hardship automatically remove responsibility.

Suppose prolonged hardship contributes to someone obtaining another person's savings through deception. Studying those conditions and understanding the hardship does not alter the victim's loss of property, security and future choice. An ethical response may combine protection and compensation, appropriate accountability, prevention of further harm and improvement of conditions that foster such conduct.

**Compassion is not permissiveness. Responsibility need not presuppose hatred.** Understanding an offender and protecting victims can express the same ethical concern.

The degree of responsibility also requires examining understanding, control, feasible alternatives, intention and actual consequences. A causal account does not alone settle these judgements. See [Moral Responsibility](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-responsibility/).

### 9.4 Multi-agent ethics goes beyond one agent's target

The CF approach is useful here. Ethical judgement must identify who acts, who is affected, who has decision authority, who bears risk, whose feasible space expands or contracts and how effects evolve across horizons.

This includes disadvantaged parties, victims, bystanders and those affected later—not only the strongest voices.

A path being effective for its participant does not automatically make it ethically acceptable. Are effects reliable? Who bears the costs? Is anyone harmed or excluded?

## 10. Current status of the outline

The connecting line is:

**Generative conditions → understanding people → explicit value commitments → multi-agent ethical judgement → responsible action and feedback.**

This cannot be read as “knowing generative conditions necessarily produces compassion or knowledge of justice”. Emotional changes vary, and ethical judgements require explicit values and independent argument.

What exists is a **philosophical outline open to further development**: more than personal impressions, but not a completed and closed system.

Further work concerns reasons for value premises; relations among freedom, responsibility and causal conditions; multi-agent conflict; and judgements that withstand cases and counterexamples. Individual parameters, psychological mechanisms and historical comparisons can be studied separately without turning this article into a research diary.

## 11. Relationship to related work

Connecting generative explanation, epistemic discipline and candidate values does not replace existing arguments in their respective fields. Hume's is–ought problem, the capability approach and work on moral responsibility examine different steps; they do not jointly prove this outline.

- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Metaethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/metaethics/): the meaning of moral propositions and grounds for moral judgement.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Hume's Moral Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral/): especially transitions from facts to oughts.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Moral Responsibility](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-responsibility/): freedom, control, causal determination and responsibility.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: The Capability Approach](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/): real freedom, developmental opportunities and value assessment.
