## One-page guide: How can two workstations hand off a part on the same day if their hours never overlap?

Imagine two workstations whose hours do not overlap. One processes parts in the morning, and the other inspects them in the afternoon. The original handoff requires both stations to be ready at the same time, and there is nowhere to hold parts in between. Each station can do its own work, but they cannot complete the handoff that day.

The two stations set aside a staging spot within the space they already have and agree on how to label, store and collect parts. A part finished in the morning can wait there until the afternoon. The work hours stay the same. **What the handoff requires has changed.** The two steps that once had to happen together can now happen in sequence.

### What this paper proposes

**An arrangement created by one activity can shape the conditions of the next.** In this example, the staging spot provides temporary storage and the handoff rules make it reusable. To see whether the changed constraints alter the feasible paths, hold the goal, deadline and external conditions fixed and compare which paths are available with and without the arrangement.

## What keeps the new path open?

A one-off shift change can solve today’s handoff. Tomorrow, both stations would still have to be ready at once. If the staging spot and handoff rules keep working, they can change the conditions for every handoff of the same kind.

Changing constraints can bring new ones. The staging spot has limited capacity, parts can only be kept for so long, and labels have to be reliable. If the spot fills up, the immediate question becomes whether space can be freed before the deadline. The same arrangement can open some paths and close others by taking up space or resources.

## How can we tell whether the available paths have changed?

Draw the handoff steps with and without the arrangement. Include the resources needed to set it up, maintain it and keep space available. Then ask three questions.

1. **Which condition changed?** Both stations once had to be ready at the same time. A part can now be stored and collected later.
2. **Which path opened or closed as a result?** With no overlap in work hours, morning processing followed by afternoon inspection is now feasible on the same day.
3. **What keeps that path open?** It takes enough storage capacity, recognisable labels and a reliable collection rule.

If the existing way of handing off parts succeeds more often, record how much its success rate improves. If a handoff that could not previously meet the deadline becomes feasible, identify how the new arrangement changed the available path.

## Research background and references

This paper takes one question from the earlier *Constraints, Paths, Feedback and Emergence* and develops a way to compare conditions before and after an arrangement changes. HCC compares how many controllable outcomes different modules and interfaces can generate. This paper traces how an established arrangement changes the feasible paths for the next round of activity. [The Hierarchical Causal–Control Hypothesis (HCC)](https://sl-knowledge.github.io/knowledge-site-en/framework/foundations/hierarchical-causal-control)

Niche construction concerns the selection pressures that follow changes to the environment. Closure of constraints in biological organisation also requires constraints to depend on and maintain one another. These provide points of comparison. The workstation example concerns how physical space and rules of use jointly change a handoff.
