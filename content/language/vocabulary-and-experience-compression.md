## 1. How can one word accommodate many experiences?

Trees differ in size, color, form, seasonal state and relations to people. Yet in conversation, “tree” often identifies roughly what we mean.

A word does not copy every tree’s details. It omits differences while retaining usable distinctions. “Meet under the tree” seldom requires a leaf count; studying disease requires species, tissue and environment, far beyond “tree.”

“Experience compression” therefore first has a functional meaning: **using less expression to call up experiential structure adequate for current understanding, communication or action.** Information loss is allowed; the question is which differences can be omitted and which change a judgment.

Shorter expression is not necessarily deeper understanding. “This is a good company” is brief but may distinguish nothing useful. A longer explanation of the conditions under which it generates cash can convey more. Compression’s value depends on the information–task relation, not word count alone.

Compression also has a strict information-theoretic meaning, requiring encoding, distributions, costs and errors. [Zaslavsky and colleagues’ color-naming study](https://www.nogsky.com/publication/2018a-pnas/2018a-PNAS.pdf) uses an information-bottleneck model to examine vocabulary complexity versus precision against cross-language data. This is a testable example in a specific domain, not direct evidence that all vocabulary, concepts and language origins follow one optimization principle.

## 2. World, experience, concept and word are distinct levels

At least four things must be distinguished: real objects and processes; experience of them; conceptual structures for recognition, inference and action; and word forms used in communication.

A particular tree is not the word “tree.” Knowledge of trees is not a word’s shape or pronunciation. Two speakers hearing the same word may coordinate successfully on a task while holding different knowledge, associations and standards.

Nor are words and concepts in fixed one-to-one correspondence. English *bank* can refer to a financial institution or a river’s edge, requiring contextual interpretation. Similar meanings have alternative expressions; a concept can be expressed by a sentence rather than one word.

The original chain “experience samples → similarity clustering → concepts → vocabulary” suggests one formation path, not the universal order of concept learning. A word may be learned first, with explanations, examples and correction building understanding. “Property rights” and “imaginary number” cannot be mastered merely by grouping similar visible objects.

A better working picture is cyclical: **experience and action build distinctions; existing words and explanations guide attention; successful use, misunderstanding and counterexamples prompt revision.** Language organizes experience while depending on perception, practice and social conditions beyond language.

## 3. Concepts organize relations as well as similarity

“Tree” suggests a feature list: roots, trunk, branches, leaves and growth. These aid recognition but are not automatically necessary and sufficient conditions for ordinary classification. A leafless winter tree remains a tree; whether a drawing counts depends on whether the task concerns an object or its image.

Prototype and family-resemblance research helps explain typical and marginal category members. [Rosch and Mervis’s experiments](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~rapaport/Papers/Papers.by.Others/RoschMervis1975.pdf) examine shared attributes, typicality and learning. They do not imply that every concept has fuzzy boundaries or that resemblance can replace legal or mathematical definitions.

Similarity is not all of semantic organization. Understanding “buy” involves who receives goods, who pays, the counterpart and the transaction. “Sell” can foreground another participant in the same transaction.

[FrameNet’s project description](https://berkeleyfn.framenetbr.ufjf.br/WhatIsFrameNet) connects lexical meanings to semantic frames and participant roles, including transactions. A word can call up relations and expectations, not only an object image.

Experience compression can therefore organize objects, participants, conditions and consequences into reusable structures, not merely group similar things. Pronunciation and a translation are entry points; recognizing roles and use conditions in actual situations is closer to mastery.

## 4. Verbs express change, but word classes do not classify the world

The original attention to verbs is worth retaining. “Break,” “absorb” and “transform” select communicable processes from continuous change. “The glass cracked” foregrounds change without necessarily specifying a cause; “He knocked the glass and cracked it” adds an agent and causation.

Distinguish lexical content from background inference. “Growth” does not always mean movement from simplicity to complexity; “stability” needs an object, disturbances and horizon. Unpacking a word into a process structure begins analysis; its surface meaning does not yield the full mechanism.

Nouns, verbs and adjectives can guide observation but cannot strictly equal objects, changes and dimensions. “Explosion” is a noun denoting an event; “know” can be a verb without expressing a transition. Languages’ grammatical categories cannot be forced into one physical-variable table.

Abstract transfer particularly needs discipline. “Flow,” “constraint” and “feedback” can support cross-domain questions, but their measurable objects and mechanisms differ in finance, organizations and physics. Calling two phenomena feedback does not establish identical dynamics.

Useful questions are: What does the word denote here? Which relations does it preserve? Which conditions does it omit? In another example, does it predict anything or merely supply familiar wording?

## 5. How does vocabulary become a shared resource?

Personal generalizations of experience are not yet communal vocabulary. Shared use requires coordination among pronunciation, writing, contexts and usage.

Coordination does not require identical mental representations. Sufficiently consistent reference, explanation or action can support communication. Conversely, a shared word can conceal disagreement: both parties may say “fairness” while applying different distributive principles.

Interaction, teaching, texts, institutions and intergenerational transmission can maintain and alter meanings. Conventions spare newcomers from renegotiating everything, but new uses, misunderstanding, group differentiation and institutional change also revise them. “Group interaction” alone cannot exclude power, education and communication channels.

[Kirby, Cornish and Smith’s iterated-learning experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2504810/) demonstrates possible cumulative structural change in artificial languages transmitted along controlled chains. Cultural transmission is experimentally approachable; this is neither a replay of all natural-language history nor proof of the original synergetic explanation.

Shared vocabulary makes experience indirectly usable through explanation and cooperation. Warnings can be understood without personally encountering every danger, but transmission can also retain errors. Words can carry experience without making the judgments they carry reliable.

## 6. How far can the order-parameter and attractor analogies go?

The original note uses synergetics to express two intuitions: many details can be summarized by fewer stable patterns, and shared patterns can influence subsequent individual usage. These generate questions, but three levels must be distinguished.

| Level | Permissible use here | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional description | Words summarize experience and coordinate understanding and action | All words achieve optimal compression |
| Working analogy | Some concepts or conventions resemble stable organizing patterns | A word is literally a physical order parameter |
| Formal modeling and testing | Define states, update rules and measurable macrovariables; compare predictions | Terminological similarity establishes universal laws |

“Concepts as attractors” suggests studying how ambiguity settles on an interpretation. But easier recognition of typical members and convergence of trajectories to an attractor are different claims. The latter requires a state space, dynamics, attraction region and stability, plus links to behavioral data.

Likewise, calling a linguistic pattern an order parameter requires deriving a macrovariable from local interactions, stating its predictions and showing why it improves on frequency or similarity models. Cognitive processing, word use and group convention occupy different levels; connections cannot be omitted.

The retained position is: **vocabulary can be studied as shared symbolic access to conceptual structures; some stable patterns merit investigation through dynamical or synergetic models.** Strict models require additional evidence, not more physics terminology.

## 7. Implications for language learning

If words call up relations and expectations, learning should go beyond word–translation pairs. For a use, prepare typical scenes, confusable neighbors, counterexamples and changes of focus under alternative wording.

For *absorb*, compare a sponge taking up water, a material absorbing energy and an organization absorbing added costs. Connections can be discussed, but collocations, mechanisms and extension boundaries must be learned separately. A shared translation does not license unrestricted substitution.

For *buy* and *sell*, diagram buyer, seller, goods and payment, then describe the transaction from different participants’ perspectives. The aim is usable meaning in context, not a hidden formula universal to all words.

This also does not establish that English is inherently abstract while Chinese only lists actions. Both can express complex relations through words, phrases and context. Comparison must specify senses, constructions, registers and tasks. Lacking an equally short word does not mean lacking the concept.

These suggestions fit the working picture, but effects need comparison. Greater engagement, more examples and longer practice can all improve performance; not every gain belongs to “order-parameter learning.”

## 8. How can the idea be tested?

Rather than announce a completed universal theory of vocabulary, choose a narrow scope and let explanations risk failure.

| Research question | Feasible comparison | What would narrow the claim? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does scene-and-relation teaching improve transfer? | Compare translation-and-example learning with similar duration, example counts and word frequency; test unseen scenes and delayed performance | Only taught examples are retained; new scenes do not improve |
| Does the proposed concept structure predict judgments? | Prespecify features or roles; test classification, ambiguity and error types on held-out material | Simple similarity or frequency baselines do equally well |
| How do shared conventions form? | Control networks, transmission and tasks; track consistency and change | Convergence occurs only with prescribed answers and disappears without training |
| Is a dynamical model necessary? | Specify dynamic predictions; compare static classification, repeated measurements and parameter stability | Only retrospective fit, without predicting transitions or new data |

Clusters in word vectors are observations about a computational model, not proof that human concepts are attractors. Consistent usage does not imply identical understanding. Each measure must identify whether it tests expression, judgment, behavior or a particular model.

Counterexamples are valuable: concepts formed through verbal explanation alone, strictly defined terms, disputed value words and persistent misunderstandings from polysemy locate where experience compression explains something and where other mechanisms are needed.

The value of this direction is making familiar words questionable again: **Which experiences and practices give them content? Which distinctions do they retain? How are they shared? When must they be unpacked, corrected or replaced?**
