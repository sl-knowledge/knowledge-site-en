# To Be Is to Be Distinct: Existence, Distinction, Relation and Meaning

_Cosmic Awakening · Fundamental Philosophical Questions · Part 0.01_

> **Thesis:** To be is to be distinct. A thing exists if and only if there is another thing from which it is distinct; distinction is itself an obtaining relation. A relation is a class of pairs composed of existent items. A thing's meaning is given by the pairs, within obtaining relations, in which it appears. From these definitions it follows that a thing exists if and only if its meaning is non-empty. This essay uses only two undefined ontological terms: **distinction** and **obtains**. Every other term is defined using only logical terms, set-theoretic terms and terms defined before it.

---

## 1. The Problem: Where a System Begins

Every system must have a starting point, and that starting point cannot itself be proved from something more basic within the system. The criteria for choosing a starting point can therefore only be these:

1. **As few terms as possible.** The fewer the undefined terms, the more fully everything that follows can be checked.
2. **As precise as possible.** It should be possible to decide whether a case falls under a term.
3. **Able to exclude something.** A starting point that excludes nothing says nothing.
4. **Able to yield something.** Non-trivial conclusions should follow from it.

These roughly correspond to Carnap's criteria for an “explication” of a concept: similarity, exactness, fruitfulness and simplicity (*Logical Foundations of Probability*, 1950).

**Rule for writing this essay:** every term appearing in a definition must belong to one of three classes:

1. an undefined term;
2. a term already defined before it;
3. a variable being introduced in that definition (x, y, R and so on).

**Undefined terms** fall into three groups:

| Group | Terms | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Logical terms | not, and, or, if ... then, if and only if, all, there is, identical | Tools shared by all rigorous writing; they make no ontological assertion |
| Set-theoretic terms | class, membership | Likewise. “Item,” “pair,” “first term” and “second term” are not separately assumed; all are defined from these two terms |
| Ontological terms | **distinction**, **obtains** | The genuine starting points of this essay. The system's assertions about the world begin with these two terms |

**Item.** An item is a class capable of belonging to some class. (That is, what is ordinarily called a “set.”)

> Terms used: class, membership.

Why distinguish a class from an item? Some classes are too large to belong to any class—for example, the class of all items. Treating that class as an item would produce paradoxes of the “set of all sets” kind. The variables x and y in this essay therefore range only over items. Classes can be discussed, but need not be items. (This formulation was found in the course of the research: the class–set theories of von Neumann 1925 and Gödel 1940.)

**Formal system used.** This essay uses such a class–set theory and includes the axiom of foundation (no item belongs to itself). Statements may quantify over classes—for example, “there is a relation R”—but classes are not collected into a larger class. “Distinction” is a primitive predicate between items; “obtains” is a primitive predicate applying to classes.

**Order of definition:**

item → distinction (Rules 1 and 2) → existence (Rule 3; Axiom 1: non-emptiness) → pair → first term, second term → relation → obtains (Axiom 2: the distinction relation obtains) → position → meaning

The sections follow this order: distinction in Section 2, existence in Section 3, relation in Section 4 and meaning in Section 5.

## 2. Distinction

**Characterisation:** x is distinct from y: x really is not y (where x and y are both items).

**Status:** an undefined ontological term and the philosophical starting point of this essay.

**Distinction and non-identity.** Distinction entails non-identity, but need not be identical to non-identity:

- **Rule 1 (entails non-identity):** if x is distinct from y, then x is not y.
- The converse does not necessarily follow: two non-identical items need not be distinct.
- **Rule 2 (symmetry):** if x is distinct from y, then y is distinct from x.
- **Rule 3 (any two existents are distinct):** if x and y both exist, and x is not y, then x is distinct from y. (“Existence” is defined in Section 3; this rule takes effect only after that definition.)

Why is symmetry needed? If only a were distinct from b, but b were not distinct from a, then under the definition in Section 3, a would exist and b would not. Yet the distinction relation would contain (a, b), even though a relation can be composed only of existent items; distinction itself would then cease to be a relation.

Why is Rule 3 needed? Without it, the following could occur: a is distinct from b, c is distinct from d, and all four exist, yet a and c are non-identical without being distinct. That conflicts with point 3 below: any two existent things are distinct simply by being two. Rule 3 does not make mathematical objects exist automatically, because its antecedent already requires both things to exist.

Together, the three rules say: **among existents, distinction and non-identity coincide completely; the additional content of distinction lies only in which items exist.**

**Corollary (provable after existence is defined in Section 3): x is distinct from y if and only if x and y both exist and x is not y.**

- Forward direction: distinction entails non-identity (Rule 1); by symmetry (Rule 2), x and y are each distinct from some item, so both exist.
- Reverse direction: Rule 3.

This corollary shows that, within this system, “distinction” and “existence” can be defined from one another: once distinction is given, it determines which items exist; once the existent items are given, distinction is just non-identity among them. This is the formal content of “convertibility” (Section 3): neither is reduced to the other; they are two ways of stating the same fact.

Two points must be made clear:

1. This system does **not** explain why there are existents, or why these particular things exist. It says only that “there are existents” and “there are distinctions” are the same fact. “Why is there something rather than nothing?” remains a central philosophical question and is not answered by a definition.
2. The system **permits**, but does not **require**, an item to be non-identical to another item while failing to exist. The claim is only that set theory cannot automatically decide which items exist.

The world's specific structure—which items stand in which relations—is not given by “distinction,” but by other obtaining relations (Section 4).

Why must the account take this form? If distinction were simply identical to non-identity, then “there is a y distinct from x” would hold for every item (for every set, another non-identical set can be found). Existence would collapse into “being an item,” and mathematical objects would be declared to exist automatically by set theory. Non-identity is a relation in the formal apparatus; distinction is a relation in the world. Set theory is a tool for talking about existents, not a list of them.

The substantive content of this essay lies in two claims:

- existence and distinction are convertible (Section 3);
- distinction is a relation that obtains in the world, not merely one that can be written down (Section 4, Axiom 2).

**Four clarifications:**

1. **Distinction needs no one to distinguish.** Anything that distinguishes must first differ from what it distinguishes; otherwise, it could distinguish nothing. Distinction therefore precedes any distinguisher (the regress argument). Moreover, before any knower appeared, the material universe already had a history of more than ten billion years filled with distinctions (the temporal argument).
2. **Distinction must be separated from distinguishing.** Distinction belongs to existence itself; **distinguishing** is a representation of distinction made by some structure: eyes discriminate colours, instruments display values, and thought separates concepts. Conceptual vagueness and arbitrary classification belong to distinguishing and do not affect distinction itself.
3. **Distinction does not require a qualitative difference (numerical distinctness).** Two existent things are distinct simply by being two (Rule 3); they need not differ in some property. Black's (1952) two qualitatively identical iron spheres are two and therefore distinct. Conversely, two formally different items—for example, two sets—are not distinct merely because they are non-identical; this essay does not decide in advance whether they exist.
4. **Distinction requires only “at least two.”** It does not presuppose that the world is discrete, has sharp boundaries, or consists of exactly two basic states.

**Why “two.”** Not because the world is made of two kinds of material, but because distinction has two ends: x is not y. Yin is yin only in relation to yang. “Two” names the two ends of distinction, not the number of kinds of entity.

## 3. Existence

**Definition (thesis):** x exists if and only if x is an item and there is an item y such that x is distinct from y.

> Terms used: item, there is, distinction.

**Status:** this is a substantive ontological thesis, not a theorem of logic. Standard first-order logic permits a domain containing only one item; this thesis says that such an isolated item does not exist.

**“The whole” is not an existent.** Let S be the class of all existent items. There are two cases:

- If S is too large to be an item, then by definition it is not an existent, because only an item can exist.
- If S is an item, then if S existed, S would have to belong to itself (because it contains every existent item). But the axiom of foundation forbids any item from belonging to itself. Therefore S does not exist.

In either case, S is not an existent. “All distinctions” and “all existent items” are plural expressions; they do not designate another existent called “the whole.” This follows from the definition and the axiom of foundation and requires no additional thesis.

**Axiom 1 (non-emptiness): there is at least one pair of items mutually distinct from one another.**

Without this axiom, a situation containing no existents would satisfy all the definitions and rules. The theorem in Section 5 would still hold, but only vacuously. This axiom says that there are existents in the world, and therefore at least two. Its basis is fact, not definition: the person writing this sentence and the sentence itself are already two distinct things.

**Convertibility.** This essay does not reduce existence to something more basic. It says that being an existent and being distinct from something else are two ways of stating the same fact. The medieval doctrine that being and one are convertible has exactly this structure.

**Exclusions:**

- **The simple One.** A completely undifferentiated “One,” with no internal distinction and nothing outside it from which it differs, is distinct from nothing and therefore does not exist.
- **A bare substratum without any distinction.** The “pure substratum” said by traditional substance theory to remain after every property has been removed does not exist.
- **“The whole” as an existent.** It does not exist, under either of the two cases above.

## 4. Relation

The definition of relation requires a prior definition of “pair.” The next three definitions use only “class,” “item” and “membership.”

**Pair.** The pair of x and y, written (x, y), is an item whose members are exactly {x} and {x, y}; these may be the same. Here {x} is the item to which only x belongs, and {x, y} is the item to which only x and y belong. (Kuratowski, 1921)

> Terms used: item, membership, only, and.

**First term.** The first term of a pair is the item that belongs to every member of that pair.

> Terms used: pair, membership, every.

**Second term.** The second term of a pair is an item y such that the members of the pair are exactly {the first term} and {the first term, y}.

> Terms used: pair, first term, item, membership, only.

Thus, when x and y are non-identical, (x, y) and (y, x) are two different items. Their “order” is given by the construction itself and need not be separately defined. When x and y are identical, the second term is the first term and the definition still holds. This case must be retained: a thing's relation to itself will be needed later when self-knowledge is discussed.

**Relation.** A relation is a class of pairs composed of existent items.

> Terms used: class, pair, existence (Section 3).

Clarifications:

- Every relation in this essay is a binary relation.
- A relation is a class, not necessarily an item. A small relation is an item and can itself be one end of another relation; an excessively large relation is only a class.
- **Distinction is itself a relation:** the class composed of all pairs of mutually distinct items.

**Obtains.** An undefined ontological term applying to an entire relation: to say that a relation obtains is to say that it is a relation actually present in the world, not merely one that can be written down.

**Rule: obtaining need not pass to subclasses.** The fact that a relation obtains does not guarantee that each of its parts obtains. Some parts may obtain, but each must do so in its own right; it does not follow from the obtaining of the whole. For example, the distinction relation obtains. But if that implied that every subclass of it also obtained, then every class of pairs assembled from different items would “obtain,” and “obtains” would lose all content.

Why can “obtains” not be defined as “this pair belongs to this relation”? Because that is only class membership, and every arbitrarily assembled class of pairs satisfies it. This is the core of Newman's problem (Objection 3 in Section 9).

**Axiom 2: the distinction relation obtains.** This is the relational content of the thesis that to be is to be distinct: distinction is not merely writable; it is actually present in the world. By the symmetry rule in Section 2, both ends of every pair in the distinction relation exist, so the distinction relation satisfies the definition of a relation.

**Position.** The position of x in a relation R is the class composed of those pairs in R in which x appears as first or second term.

> Terms used: first term, second term, pair, membership, relation, class.

Here, position is a **concrete** position: it records the pairs in which x appears and the end at which it occurs, rather than the abstract structural role played by x.

**Relata do not precede relations.** This and that do not first exist as independent things and then become connected by a relation. Under Section 3, being an existent item and occupying one end of the distinction relation are the same fact.

**Described and obtaining relations.** A novel describes a unicorn: the novel exists and the description it gives exists (this is a distinction belonging to representation), but the unicorn does not. A described relation does not obtain merely because it is described.

**Possibility.** An unrealised possibility is in no obtaining relation and is therefore not an existent. What statements about “possibility” mean—for example, “this stone could break”—requires a prior account of laws and feasibility and is left to a separate essay.

## 5. Meaning

**Definition:** for any relation R and any pair p, **R and p constitute one meaning condition of x** if and only if R obtains, p belongs to R, and x is the first or second term of p. The meaning of x is all the meaning conditions of x.

> Terms used: relation, pair, obtains, membership, first term, second term, all.

Formally, this is a ternary condition M(x; R, p), not a pair. It cannot be written as (R, p), because R may be a proper class, whereas a pair can be composed only of items.

**Why not write “the class composed of all positions.”** The position of x in a large relation may itself be a proper class, and a proper class cannot in turn be collected as a member of another class. Meaning is therefore not defined as a collection, but as a condition: it says only which R and p constitute a meaning condition of x, neither packaging them as a pair nor collecting them into a larger class. The position of x in any one relation R remains a subclass of R and can legitimately be written down (Section 4).

**Theorem: x exists if and only if the meaning of x is non-empty.**

“The meaning of x is non-empty” means that there is an R and a p that constitute one meaning condition of x.

- **Forward direction.** x exists, so there is a y distinct from x. By symmetry, y also exists, so (x, y) belongs to the distinction relation. Because the distinction relation obtains (Section 4, Axiom 2), the distinction relation and (x, y) constitute one meaning condition of x. Its meaning is therefore non-empty.
- **Reverse direction.** There are an obtaining R and a p belonging to R, and x is the first or second term of p. A relation is composed only of existent items, so x exists.

**Status:** a theorem derived from the definitions, Rules 1 and 2 of distinction, and Axiom 2. Axiom 1 prevents it from being vacuously true. Its substantive content comes from the thesis about existence; it is not a separate discovery.

**Formal theorem and philosophical claim.** This theorem is a theorem within the formal language of this essay, secured by its definitions, rules and axioms. Any claim it makes about reality and meaning can arise only from two sources:

1. **The theses themselves.** “To be is to be distinct” and “the distinction relation obtains” are substantive claims and may be false. If they are false, the theorem remains formally valid but no longer describes the world.
2. **Adequacy of explication.** The essay's “meaning” is an explication, in Carnap's sense, of the ordinary word “meaning,” not a report of its existing usage. Its adequacy must be judged by Carnap's four criteria: similarity to the original term (see “Levels of meaning” below and the supporting evidence at the linguistic level), exactness, fruitfulness and simplicity. If the explication is inadequate, the theorem concerns only “meaning” as defined here, not meaning in its ordinary sense.

The philosophical weight of the theorem therefore equals the credibility of the two theses plus the adequacy of the explication. Each must be argued for separately; the theorem itself cannot substitute for those arguments.

**Why this is not circular.** Circularity would mean using A to prove B, B to prove C, and then C to prove A. Here there is a one-way chain of definitions (Section 1), from which the theorem follows. Existence, distinction and meaning have the same extension but different intensions:

| Term | Perspective | Question asked |
|---|---|---|
| Existence | What there is | Is it there? |
| Distinction | Between two ends | Is x not y? |
| Meaning | From one end | In which positions in which obtaining relations does this end occur? |

The transcendentals discussed in Aquinas's *De Veritate*, question 1, article 1, have the same structure: being, one, true and good have the same extension but different intensions. This essay does not include “good” among them, in order not to import value into ontology.

**Levels of meaning: the same definition, different kinds of obtaining relation.**

| Level | Meaning = position in what kind of relation? |
|---|---|
| Most basic | A position in physically obtaining relations; for example, rock becoming an environment for life |
| Experienced good and bad | The relation between an event and whether a sentient life can continue to exist |
| Linguistic meaning | Relations of a sign to other signs and to the world |
| Meaning asked about and bestowed by people | Relations of a person's present to their past, future, other people and actions |

In ordinary language, “meaning” usually refers to the higher levels. They appear only when particular structures arise, but under this definition they are the same kind of thing as meaning at the most basic level.

**Richness of meaning.** Meanings differ in amount: the more obtaining relations in which a thing stands, the richer its meaning. How “amount” and “depth” should be compared requires later concepts such as dependence and condensation and is left to later essays.

**Supporting evidence at the linguistic level.** Saussure says that in language there are only differences and that the value of a sign is determined by its relations to other signs (1916). Firth says that one can know a word by the company it keeps (1957). Wittgenstein says that the meaning of a word is its use in the language (*Philosophical Investigations*, §43).

Today's large language models learn only from relations among words, yet to a considerable degree appear to grasp meaning. This point is disputed: Bender and Koller (2020) argue that meaning cannot be learned from form alone, because meaning also includes relations between signs and the world. Under this essay's definition, their dispute is precisely about which relations count as meaning. It is therefore offered here only as supporting evidence, not as proof.

**Mathematical correspondence (not proof).** The Yoneda lemma in category theory says that, up to isomorphism, an object is completely determined by its morphisms to and from all objects. This is formally similar to “a thing's meaning is its relations,” but it is a theorem internal to category theory and is used here only as a correspondence.

**Consistency: a model.** Take two items a = ∅ and b = {∅}. Stipulate that only they are mutually distinct, so the distinction relation is {(a, b), (b, a)}, and stipulate that it obtains; for the time being, no other relation is stipulated to obtain. Then a and b both exist and both have non-empty meanings. Other sets remain formal items but do not exist and have empty meanings. All three rules, both axioms and the central theorem are satisfied. This shows that the definitions have at least one consistent interpretation; it is not a proof of the ontology of reality.

## 7. What It Excludes: Testing Hard Cases

A substantive thesis must be capable of being overturned by a counterexample. If something is found that exists but is in no obtaining relation, or that is in an obtaining relation but does not exist, this essay is wrong.

| Hard case | Result |
|---|---|
| The simple One | Nothing is distinct from it → does not exist |
| The whole (all existent items) | Either not an item, or an item that cannot belong to itself → not an existent |
| An unrealised possibility | In no obtaining relation → does not exist |
| A region of the universe beyond our horizon and forever inaccessible to us | Distinct from its own parts and situated in obtaining relations → exists and has meaning |
| A fictional character | The description exists (as representation); the character itself is in no obtaining relation → does not exist |
| The past | **Undecided.** Depends on a decision about whether “the past still obtains” (a growing block; no decision yet) |
| Numbers and the empty set | **Undecided.** Depends on a decision about the status of mathematical objects (no decision yet) |
| **Conscious experience** (held to exist while having no effect on the physical world) | **A genuine hard case.** Under this essay, either it stands in an obtaining relation—for example, to the subject's own state—and therefore exists, or it does not exist. This is a dispute over positions in the philosophy of mind and is left to a separate essay |

## 8. Consequences

1. **Under certain conditions, self-knowledge cannot be complete.** Lawvere's (1969) fixed-point theorem says that, in a system satisfying its conditions—roughly, one in which every property about the system itself can be represented by a function on some part of the system, that is, by a point-surjective map—the system cannot completely represent all properties about itself from within whenever the range of property values B admits a fixed-point-free endomorphism. Whenever B contains at least two distinct elements, such an endomorphism exists: choose a not equal to b, map a to b, and map everything else to a. This map has no fixed point.
   - What this essay supplies is “at least two mutually distinct existents.”
   - But this does not automatically imply that the value range B of some particular cognitive system contains at least two elements. The correspondence must be established separately for each system.
   - Whether a particular cognitive system satisfies the theorem's remaining conditions must be argued case by case; see Paper 1 in this series.
   - The conclusion here is therefore conditional: **every cognitive system satisfying these conditions is unable to know itself completely.** It is not an unconditional theorem that “all knowledge is incomplete.”
2. **Possible worlds are not needed as existents.** A possibility is not an existent. How statements about possibilities can be true is left to an essay on laws and feasibility.
3. **Meaning receives a unified definition.** From rocks to human beings, meaning is the same kind of thing; only the kinds and number of relations differ.

## 9. Objections and Replies

Each objection is classified first:

- **H0:** a conceptual difference that clarification can resolve;
- **H2:** a difference in standards of evaluation or basic position that will not disappear in principle;
- **H3 / technical problem:** a genuine criticism that must be answered.

**Objection 1: circularity—“without relata, there is no relation.”** (H0 → the remaining part is H2)

Distinction presupposes the existence of this and that, so defining existence through distinction is circular. This is the best-known objection to structural realism (Psillos, Chakravartty).

Reply: the chain of definitions in this essay is one-way (Section 1). The thesis about existence is a thesis of convertibility, not reduction. Being an existent and occupying one end of a distinction are the same fact. The remaining disagreement concerns whether a starting point must be reduced to something more basic and is therefore a difference in evaluative standards.

**Objection 2: Bradley's regress.** (Technical problem)

If a relation R connects a and b, another relation is needed to connect R with a and b, and so on without end (Bradley, 1893).

Reply: the regress arises only if relata precede a relation and need to be “glued” together. Here the relata are the two ends of the relation itself; there is no gap that requires gluing.

**Objection 3: Newman's problem—structure is empty talk.** (Technical problem; the strongest objection)

Any set with enough elements can be endowed with any relational structure, so saying that “the world has such-and-such a structure” states only the number of its elements (Newman, 1928).

Reply: this problem assumes that every relation that can be written down counts as part of the world's structure. This essay takes “obtains” as an undefined ontological term and stipulates that obtaining need not pass to subclasses. Many relations can be written; only some obtain. This belongs to the same family of response as Lewis's (1983) “naturalness” and Sider's (2011) “structure” (found in the course of the research). It is a **response strategy**, not a complete solution: the essay says only that some relations obtain and others do not, without identifying which ones.

**Concession:** this essay gives no criterion for deciding “which relations obtain.” That is an epistemic problem rather than a definitional one and is left for later work.

**Objection 4: existence is not a predicate.** (H0)

Kant says that existence is not a real predicate (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A598/B626).

Reply: this essay does not add a property to a concept; it gives conditions for being an existent. Quine's “no entity without identity” supports precisely this kind of condition.

**Objection 5: distinctions are drawn by the mind.** (H2)

Every distinction we know is one that we draw.

Reply: the regress and temporal arguments (Section 2).

**Concession:** that distinction is independent of mind is a position that cannot be proved from within. If a knower satisfies the conditions in Section 8.1, this agrees with the conclusion there: an internal representation cannot completely account for the whole.

**Objection 6: distinction is just “non-identity” and says nothing new.** (H0)

Reply: distinction is not identical to non-identity (Section 2). Non-identity is a relation in the formal apparatus: any two different sets satisfy it. Distinction is a relation in the world, while non-identity is only a necessary condition for it. If the two are equated, existence collapses into “being an item.” The content of this essay lies in the convertibility of existence and distinction and in the claim that the distinction relation obtains.

**Objection 7: this is too trivial to say anything.** (H2)

Reply: it excludes the simple One, unrealised possibilities, bare substrata and the whole as an existent; under certain conditions, it entails that self-knowledge cannot be complete. An account that can exclude and entail is not empty.

**Objection 8: meaning is semantic, not ontological.** (H0)

Meaning belongs to language and mind; saying that a stone “has meaning” is a misuse of the word.

Reply: this essay's most basic meaning and linguistic meaning are different levels of the same definition (Section 5); linguistic meaning itself arises from relations among signs.

## 10. Predecessors Found in Our Research

Every connection among these four concepts has been encountered by earlier thinkers:

- **Existence and otherness:** Plato's *Sophist* treats “otherness” as one of the greatest kinds running through everything and identifies the mark of being as the capacity to act or be acted upon (247e); Spinoza writes that “all determination is negation”; Hegel develops the point in *Science of Logic*.
- **Existence and difference:** Deleuze's *Difference and Repetition* (1968): difference is being itself, while identity is the result of difference.
- **Relations prior to objects:** Poincaré (1902): mathematics studies not objects but relations; Cassirer (1910): from the concept of substance to the concept of function; ontic structural realism (Ladyman and Ross, 2007); Floridi (2008): relations of difference constitute all relata; Barad (2007): relata do not precede relations.
- **Existence and meaning:** Gabriel (2015): to exist is to appear in a field of sense; Nancy (1996): existence is meaning, and existence is always “being-with.”
- **Distinction and meaning:** Luhmann's systems theory begins from Spencer-Brown's distinction and defines meaning through the distinction between the actual and the potential; for Bateson, a difference that makes a difference is the unit of information and mind.
- **Meaning and relation:** Saussure, Firth and Wittgenstein.
- **Convertibility with being:** the medieval doctrine of the transcendentals.
- **Obtaining rather than arbitrary relations:** Lewis (1983), Sider (2011).

**How this essay differs from them:**

1. **It recognises only obtaining relations.** Deleuze holds that the real includes the virtual, and Luhmann's account of meaning depends on the potential; this essay recognises only obtaining relations.
2. **Distinction does not depend on an observer.** In Spencer-Brown and Luhmann, a distinction is drawn by an observer; Bateson confines distinction to the worlds of mind and information; Barad depends on an “agential cut.” Here distinction precedes every distinguisher and belongs to all existence.
3. **“The whole” is not an existent.** Gabriel argues that “the world does not exist.” This essay reaches a similar conclusion, but derives it from its definitions and the set-theoretic axiom of foundation (Section 3).
4. **The chain of definitions can be checked term by term.** It uses two undefined ontological terms, together with logical terms and two set-theoretic terms (class and membership); everything else is defined in sequence.
5. **It connects with theorems.** Distinction supplies the “at least two values” condition for Lawvere's fixed-point theorem (the remaining conditions require separate proof); meaning formally corresponds to the Yoneda lemma.

**Why adopt this account.** Compared with several familiar criteria of existence, this essay's proposal has the following features. A complete comparative argument is left to a journal-oriented revision.

| Account | Criterion of existence | Difference from this essay |
|---|---|---|
| Quine | A theory's ontological commitments are shown by the values its bound variables must take | Quine uses the values of bound variables in a theory to identify its ontological commitments and leaves “which theory to accept” to separate considerations; this essay additionally introduces “distinction,” explicitly separating items that can be discussed formally from existents that are accepted ontologically |
| Meinong | Non-existent objects also have some kind of status | This essay gives no standing to non-existent objects: unrealised possibilities and fictional characters do not exist |
| Plato's *Sophist* | Capacity to act or be acted upon | “Action” would first have to be defined; this essay does not use it at the foundational level |
| Gabriel | Appearance in a field of sense | Meaning here does not depend on a field or a perspective, but is position in obtaining relations |

The advantages of this account are:

- the chain of definitions is one-way and can be checked term by term;
- it explicitly excludes the simple One, unrealised possibilities, bare substrata and the whole as an existent;
- existence and meaning are supplied by the same chain of definitions.

Its costs are:

- it requires two undefined ontological terms;
- it does not answer which relations obtain, or why there are existents.

## 11. Open Problems

1. **How to know which relations obtain** (the epistemic part of Newman's problem).
2. **Comparing richness of meaning:** this requires later concepts such as dependence and condensation.
3. **Conscious experience** as a hard case.
4. **The status of the past and mathematical objects:** this depends on two decisions not yet made.
5. **How statements about “possibility” can be true:** this requires a separate treatment of laws and feasibility.
