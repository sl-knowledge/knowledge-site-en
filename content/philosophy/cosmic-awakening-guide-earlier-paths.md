## Afterword: a guide to earlier thought

This essay grew from my long-standing reflections on the universe, evolution and the meaning of life. While writing, I repeatedly discussed the ideas with AI and gradually found that some resembled earlier thought, so I brought the relevant works into comparison. My ideas are also shaped by education, reading and personal experience, influences that cannot always be traced individually. The guide records the connections I can presently identify and the questions I still want to pursue from them.

### 1. Alternation between aggregation and separation

- **Laozi's “being and non-being give rise to each other”** (*Daodejing*, Chapter 2): being and non-being arise through mutual dependence. Section 1 dissolves “why something rather than nothing?” under the definition that existence is distinction; the two can be read together.
- **Empedocles** used the alternation of Love and Strife to explain aggregation and separation. This essay uses a related alternation and asks how one aggregation leaves conditions for the next. [Fragments of Empedocles](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Fragments_of_Empedocles)
- **The *Xici* commentary to the *Yijing*** presents waxing and waning yin and yang through the alternation of sun and moon, heat and cold. This essay uses condensation and expansion to ask how structures formed through alternation accumulate. [*Xici*, Part II](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/易傳/繫辭下)
- **Herbert Spencer's *First Principles*** contrasts the integration of matter and concomitant dissipation of motion with dissolution. This distinction can be read alongside the essay's account of formation, maintenance and breakdown. [*First Principles*](https://constitution.org/2-Authors/hs/first_prin.htm)

### 2. Cosmic evolution, complexity and consciousness

- **Teilhard de Chardin's *The Phenomenon of Man*** connects complexity, consciousness and the Omega Point. This essay retains a link between complexity and consciousness while keeping evolution open.
- **Whitehead's *Process and Reality*** understands reality through process and becoming, but gives God and “initial aims” explicit roles. This essay places formation, persistence and change in a philosophical picture without predetermined purpose.
- **Carl Sagan's *Cosmos*** presents humanity as one way the cosmos knows itself. This essay asks how knowledge of our situation changes action and what may happen later. [Library of Congress Sagan archive](https://www.loc.gov/static/collections/finding-our-place-in-the-cosmos-with-carl-sagan/about-this-collection/overview.html)
- **David Christian's *Maps of Time*** places cosmic, biological and human history on one timescale and studies conditions for new levels. This essay asks how formed structures are preserved and reused. [University of California Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/maps-of-time/epub-pdf)
- **Eric Chaisson's *Cosmic Evolution*** compares complexity through energy-flow density per unit mass. It offers a quantitative route; this essay poses philosophical questions through information condensation and expansion.
- **Schelling** held that nature reaches self-consciousness in humanity: “Nature is visible Spirit; Spirit is invisible Nature.” **Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit*** describes Spirit reaching absolute self-knowledge through history. Both resemble the information field knowing itself through manifestation, but this essay has no endpoint or purpose.
- **Ray Kurzweil's *The Singularity Is Near*** calls the expansion of intelligence into the universe “the universe wakes up.” This essay adopts the phrase to ask how self-knowledge relates to action.

### 3. Information and the universe

- **Poincaré** and **Worrall**: science knows only relations (epistemic structural realism). **Ladyman and Ross, *Every Thing Must Go***: the world itself consists of relations and structures (ontic structural realism). Section 1 adopts the latter position.
- **John Wheeler** explored information and physical reality through *It from Bit*.
- **Seth Lloyd** understands the universe through computation and information processing.
- **Stephen Wolfram** studies how simple programs generate complex behaviour.
- **Leibniz**, in his correspondence with Clarke, argues that time and space are relations among things rather than independent containers. This agrees with Section 1's “space is the structure of relations among events.”
- **Carlo Rovelli, *The Order of Time***, and **Julian Barbour, *The End of Time*** argue that time may not be fundamental. **Sean Carroll, *From Eternity to Here*** discusses the arrow of time and entropy. This essay distinguishes physical time from an order of dependence and relates time's direction to entropy and accumulated records.
- **Mark Van Raamsdonk** and related work on building spacetime from quantum entanglement represent an approach in which spacetime may be emergent. This agrees directionally with the priority of the information field, but remains unsettled.
- **Max Tegmark's mathematical-universe hypothesis** says that all mathematical structures exist and ours is one of them. It is close to the substrate containing all mathematical and logical possibilities. Tegmark treats all structures as equally existing. Here there is a process of manifestation: only self-sustaining structures stand, while invariant mathematics and logic and cycle-dependent physical laws produce successive evolution.

Wheeler, Lloyd and Wolfram were the three information-related sources cited in the 2025 version. This essay asks how information expands, condenses into structures, and changes conditions for later evolution.

### 4. Purposeless nature and meaning

- **Spinoza's *Ethics***, Part I, discusses projection of purpose onto nature, while Part V discusses the intellectual love of God. The first helps identify teleological misreading; together they show why the essay values both natural regularity and historical conditions.
- **Ursula Goodenough's *The Sacred Depths of Nature*** seeks awe, belonging and ethical concern within scientific nature. This essay asks how those concerns become things people preserve. [Oxford University Press](https://academic.oup.com/book/52725)
- **Sartre** emphasises how people shape themselves through choice. This essay also locates meaning in choice and places that capacity within the evolution of life and cognition.
- **Kelemen's** work finds that children more readily than adults explain natural features by their uses. It informs the discussion of human preference for purpose explanations.

### 5. Structure, selection and contingency

- **Lawvere's fixed-point theorem (1969)**: where a fixed-point-free negation exists, no system can completely represent all its own properties. **Yanofsky (2003)** explains how the results of Cantor, Russell, **Gödel (1931)**, Tarski and **Turing (1936)** are special cases. **Turing (1939)** and **Feferman (1962)** studied progressions strengthened by successive consistency statements. **Wolpert (2008)** shows that no physical inference device can predict or know everything including itself. Section 4 uses these results to argue that the distinction enabling manifestation also prevents complete knowledge.
- **Goodstein (1944)** and **Kirby and Paris (1982)**: a true arithmetic statement using only addition, subtraction and exponentiation is unprovable in Peano arithmetic and requires transfinite ordinals. It illustrates why stepping outside reveals the whole.
- **Chaitin (1974, 2005)**: information-theoretic incompleteness—finite axioms cover finite information—and the proposal that mathematics add new axioms from experience. Section 4 connects this to the unending alternation of condensation and expansion.
- **Aristotle** accepted potential but not actual infinity. **Cantor** developed transfinite numbers and defended actual infinity while distinguishing unknowable absolute infinity and three levels: material universe, human mind and God. **Zermelo (1930)** treated the set-theoretic universe as a potentially infinite ascent of actually infinite levels. **Grim, *The Incomplete Universe* (1991)** argues that there is no set of all truths and that truth leaves the universe essentially open. **Hilbert, “On the Infinite” (1926)** and **Ellis, Meissner and Nicolai (2018)** address actual infinity in physical reality. Section 4 maps three infinities onto the information field, mathematics and material universes.
- **Spencer-Brown's *Laws of Form* (1969)** constructs a logical algebra from the primitive act of drawing a distinction. This is close to “to exist is to be distinct” and the 2025 claim that two is the first bit.
- **Wheeler's resetting of constants** and **Smolin's cosmological natural selection**: Wheeler randomises constants at each bounce; Smolin transmits varying constants to daughter universes and selects through their number. Differential retention lies between them: heredity and variation, but outcome-dependent variation without population competition.
- **Stress-induced mutagenesis in bacteria** and **Rechenberg's evolution strategy** offer biological and computational analogies for raising variation under stress and reducing step size after success.
- **Borde, Guth and Vilenkin** relate past incompleteness to averaged expansion. **Fred Adams** warns against overstating how narrowly fine-tuned constants must be.
- **Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler and Hoyle (1957)** systematically explained stellar nucleosynthesis. Section 6 treats stellar generations—gravitational condensation, nucleosynthesis, explosive expansion and new condensation—as a spiral within the material universe.
- **Prigogine** showed how energy flows far from equilibrium can produce ordered dissipative structures. **Jeremy England** discusses self-replication and dissipation statistically. Both provide scientific background for order arising in energy flows.
- **Simon, “The Architecture of Complexity”**: stable intermediate components permit complex systems to be built in layers and resumed after interruption. Section 2 uses this to explain modules, hierarchical accumulation and the living seed.
- **Holling's adaptive cycle and panarchy**: ecosystems move through exploitation, conservation, release and reorganisation. Accumulation and tight coupling create rigidity; release is collapse; reorganisation builds new structures from released resources; nested slow levels provide memory for faster levels. This is close to condensation, over-condensation, destruction as expansion, re-condensation, and the ratio between destructive and module scales.
- **Schumpeter's creative destruction** describes new economic combinations replacing old structures.
- **Gould's *Wonderful Life*** and **Conway Morris's *Life's Solution*** emphasise contingency and convergence respectively. This essay separates recurrent structures under similar conditions from paths shaped by particular histories.
- **Smolin's cosmological natural selection** and **Penrose's conformal cyclic cosmology** offer different accounts of inheritance and cycles. In **Steinhardt and Turok (2006)**, the cosmological constant decreases across cycles and remains longest at small values, making it the closest physical model to differential retention.
- **Kelvin and Clausius** developed the nineteenth-century picture of cosmic heat death. The cyclic model of **Steinhardt and Turok**, like Penrose and Smolin, places universes after a universe at the physical level. This essay instead steps to the information-field level because a system cannot account for itself from within.
- **Aaronson, Carroll and Ouellette** provide a concrete coffee-automaton example of complexity rising and falling.

### 6. Meaning and ethics

- **Cummins** understands the function of a part as its role in a system. This can be read with “place in evolution.”
- **Nagel** discusses the irreducibility of animal subjective experience. **Bentham and Peter Singer** bring animal suffering into ethics. These correspond to experienced good and bad and reducing avoidable suffering.
- **Hume** argues that ought does not follow from is. This essay therefore states its ethical commitments and offers only conditional directions.
- **Hans Jonas's ethics of responsibility** and **Aldo Leopold's land ethic** stress long-term consequences for future generations and living communities, corresponding to continuable work and protection of what is hard to rebuild.
- **Popper's** fallibilism and open society correspond to keeping knowledge open.
- **Kenneth Craik** proposed that minds build small-scale models of reality to predict. This is close to Section 6's functional definition of thought.
- **Daniel Dennett's tower of generate-and-test** moves from genetic and learned trial and error to internal simulation and language-and-tool-assisted learning, closely matching heredity, learning, thought, reflection and knowledge.
- **Charles Bennett's logical depth** measures complexity by the length of the incompressible process required to generate a structure. Section 7 uses it for the depth of cosmic history.
- **Rolf Landauer's principle** gives an unavoidable thermodynamic cost to erasing information, a precise example of matter constraining thought.
- **Howard Pattee** and **Terrence Deacon** study how symbols control matter and how information and meaning constrain physical processes, close to the claim that thought structures reorganise matter through constraints.
- **Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy** shows how beliefs about a situation change it. This essay emphasises the inverse: a warning taken seriously may prevent its prediction.
- **Ćirković, Sandberg and Bostrom's anthropic shadow** concerns how observer survival limits estimates of catastrophic risk.
