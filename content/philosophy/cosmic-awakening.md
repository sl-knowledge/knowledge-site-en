## 1. Information is the essence of the universe

What is the universe fundamentally made of? **My philosophical position is that information is the essence of the universe, and matter is one form that information takes under particular regularities.**

In this framework, information refers broadly to existence, relationships, structures and possibilities for change. This sense is broader than the knowledge people record and communicate or the quantity measured in Shannon’s theory of communication. I use this philosophical position to understand existence and how it evolves.

This essay is a concise revision of my 2025 work *Cosmic Awakening Information Field Theory*.

From this starting point, the next questions are how information evolves and how it takes the form of matter, life, cognition and such a diverse world. This leads to information expansion and condensation.

## 2. Information expansion and condensation

The alternation of information expansion and condensation is like the breathing of the universe.

- **Information expansion is like breathing out.** A structure formed through condensation comes back into contact with the world and encounters a wider range of situations. A scientific theory, for example, encounters new phenomena and opens new possibilities when applied to new questions. This unfolding of possibilities and diversity is what the original essay calls “information growth.”
- **Information condensation is like breathing in.** Dispersed information forms relatively stable structures. Science condenses many observations into theories, and civilizations condense complex experiences into knowledge that can be passed on.

The universe has no purpose. Relatively stable structures are more likely than scattered information to persist and be passed on. What survives can sometimes look as though it formed in pursuit of a purpose.

Evolution can then be understood as a recurring cycle.

> **Information expansion → new possibilities unfold → information condensation → another round of expansion.**

The results of past evolution become the starting point for what follows. Information condensation preserves the past and can help create the future.

Within its own timescale, a single system can alternate between condensation and expansion. Across the vast universe, condensation is happening in some places while expansion is happening in others. The essay envisages a similar rhythm for the universe as a whole over longer timescales.

## 3. Entropy, creation and disintegration

Living things use energy to sustain their internal activity while releasing heat and waste into their surroundings. The order within a living system and the dissipation around it are two aspects of the same process. When discussing an increase in entropy, we have to consider the system together with its surroundings. [Prigogine’s Nobel Lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/prigogine-lecture.pdf)

A structure has a life cycle from emergence and flourishing to eventual disintegration. Its manifest complexity can rise and then fall, and some of what it produced may be carried forward by new structures. The coffee automaton study gives an example of complexity rising and falling in a particular model under a specified measure. See [Aaronson, Carroll and Ouellette (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.6903).

A changeless universe whose structures lasted forever might be a magnificent but cold “crystal prison.” If everything were permanently fixed, there would be no new events, relationships or creations.

The breakdown of old structures sometimes reopens possibilities that had been closed off.

### Building, destruction and protection

Condensation and expansion in themselves carry no moral judgment. Scientific theories condense knowledge, and ways of harming others can also be accumulated and refined. Education spreads knowledge, and deception can spread too. A manuscript can be lost to decay or deliberately burned. To judge the moral significance, we also have to ask what people chose to do and who was harmed.

Building and destruction can happen at the same time. Repeated destruction can compound losses, and capabilities that once existed can be lost. If people elsewhere can still practise and teach those capabilities, or if surviving records and tools are sufficient for others to learn them again, there is a chance to resume the work. **I call what still allows people to start again after disaster a “living seed.”** Over long periods, people in many fields have gained access to more knowledge and become able to explore what earlier generations could not. The direction I am describing can be seen in these changes in what people can know and do.

People also learn from destruction how to protect what they have built. The 1954 Hague Convention requires its parties to prepare in peacetime to safeguard important books, manuscripts and archives. During the 2012–2013 conflict in Mali, some of Timbuktu’s manuscripts were lost, and local residents moved around 300,000 manuscripts out of harm’s way. These examples show two aspects of protection: turning lessons into rules that later generations can use, and taking action to save what matters when danger arrives. [Hague Convention](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/convention-protection-cultural-property-event-armed-conflict-regulations-execution-convention?hub=416) · [Timbuktu heritage assessment](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/damage-timbuktus-cultural-heritage-worse-first-estimated-reports-unesco-mission)

## 4. Cycles do more than repeat

A cosmic cycle begins again under conditions left by what came before, so the starting point changes.

Every set of structures and rules opens some possibilities and closes others. When old structures grow rigid, adjustments within the existing rules often bring about less change. Further changes may become possible when the rules themselves change.

The original essay calls this process the “Gödelian spiral.” Gödel’s incompleteness theorem serves here as a philosophical inspiration and a structural analogy. A set of rules can leave problems it cannot resolve, and the next round can encounter limits of its own.

Following this analogy, I am inclined to believe that the universe will keep evolving, one cycle after another. Each cycle inherits conditions from its predecessors and leaves new conditions for what follows. In this philosophical picture, there is no first cycle and no last. **It has neither a beginning nor an end.**

The same analogy applies to human understanding. Sometimes a difficulty persists because our concepts, goals or ways of observing set a boundary on what we can see. Solving it may require stepping back from the system to reconsider how it is framed.

## 5. Two layers of information: what happens and what remains

The original essay uses two layers of information to think about cosmic cycles.

The first is the world as it unfolds within a cycle. Possibilities become events, giving rise to galaxies, life, thought and civilization. It is the universe’s “sandbox” for that cycle.

The second is the historical information left by successive cycles. In this picture, what a universe has gone through condenses at the end of a cycle into a kind of “archive of wisdom,” which shapes the conditions for the next round of evolution.

The question concerns what the archive could carry forward, whatever physical form it might take.

> **Can the past enter the future in some form so that the next round need not start from scratch?**

We already see patterns like this in life, culture and knowledge. Heredity allows living things to inherit the past. Speech and writing allow civilizations to preserve experience. Theories give later generations a starting point for further inquiry.

Extending the pattern to cosmic cycles is a philosophical conjecture. “Memory” here means that structures left by the past continue to affect what follows.

Even if the universe as a whole follows the rhythm envisaged here, a particular place, such as Earth, could come to an end and the knowledge accumulated there could be lost. Whether Earth’s experience could be carried forward elsewhere or at a later stage of evolution remains an open question.

## 6. Life, death and the meaning of existence

This view of the universe leads to a reconsideration of the meaning of existence. From stars and rocks to animals and humans, “meaning” can refer to four different things.

1. **An assigned purpose.** The universe has no predetermined purpose. People can set purposes for themselves in their own lives.
2. **A place in evolution.** Stars formed and released some of the elements that would later make up planets and living things. Rocks and minerals make up part of the environment in which life exists, and plants store the sun’s energy in forms living things can use. In this sense, all things have their place. Many structures that formed before us became conditions for later life. [NASA on the life cycle of stars](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/star-lifecycle/)
3. **What feels good or bad to a being.** What it means for a sentient being to encounter the world has to be understood through its own experience. Animals can feel pain and pleasure even when they cannot use the word “meaning.”
4. **Questioning and giving meaning.** People use language to organise experience. They can look back on their lives, imagine the future and, knowing that they will die, ask what they cherish and how they want to live. We routinely ask what things are for. We can ask the same question about natural objects and eventually about ourselves. [Kelemen’s study of children’s teleological explanations](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.35.6.1440)

These senses of meaning can be summed up in one sentence.

> **All things have a place in evolution. Sentient beings have experiences that feel good or bad to them. Human beings are the part of the universe that has begun to ask about meaning and give it to things.**

Time and energy can give people room to think more deeply about these questions, and hardship can make them more urgent. When people ask why their own lives matter, they are also re-examining the concepts and values through which they understand the world. This is the move described in Section 4, from working within a system to reflecting on its framing, applied to one’s own existence.

Life is a structure formed in the course of evolution that can perceive, remember, learn and create. With human beings, a part of the universe began to ask about the origins, structure and meaning of the universe itself.

### Was the appearance of humans inevitable or contingent?

Stephen Jay Gould imagined rewinding the tape of life and playing it again. On his account, humans would probably fail to reappear. Simon Conway Morris emphasised convergent evolution, in which different lineages sometimes develop similar structures independently. Their disagreement helps us distinguish two questions. Can a type of structure recur under similar conditions? How much of the particular course that led to humans on Earth could be replayed? [Conway Morris’s *Life’s Solution*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/lifes-solution/82BFF629ABD29A44F1B428D03C094102)

My view is that **given conditions can make some outcomes more likely, and what actually emerges still depends on what has already happened.** Under certain conditions, structures that can withstand disturbances are more likely to persist. When the environment changes, a structure that was once stable may lose the conditions that sustained it. Humans are one particular path taken by evolution on Earth.

With human beings, language, writing and tools allow much of what people learn to pass between individuals and across generations. Some knowledge can be revised within a single generation. By following the history of human knowledge, we can observe how what survives changes the range of things later generations can encounter and explore.

We observe from within the universe. Our thoughts, feelings, science, art and creations belong to its unfolding history.

The original essay expresses the cycle of life and death in a deliberately strong sentence.

> **Life is for the sake of death, and death is for the sake of life.**

Here “for the sake of” expresses how life and death shape one another within the cycle. To live is to write a unique chapter, and death brings that particular life to a close. A life can end and still leave its mark on other lives, relationships, knowledge, works of art and civilization. Some of what it leaves can become part of later structures.

The meaning of life extends beyond resisting its end. It can lie in living out its finite course as fully, deeply and creatively as we can. The small cycles of our lives and deaths take part in the world’s longer processes of creation and change.

“Cosmic Awakening” can be understood in this sense, even if the universe as a whole has no humanlike consciousness.

> **The universe has given rise to beings capable of knowing, understanding and reflecting on it. A part of the universe has begun to know the universe itself.**

Carl Sagan expressed a closely related idea in *Cosmos*. [The Library of Congress Sagan archive](https://www.loc.gov/static/collections/finding-our-place-in-the-cosmos-with-carl-sagan/about-this-collection/overview.html)

## 7. What should we do?

Knowing how the world evolves answers a question of fact. To say how we ought to act, we also have to say what we value. Hume reminds us to explain the reasoning that takes us from facts to what ought to be done. My starting point is this: **the suffering of humans and other sentient beings deserves concern, other people’s choices deserve respect, and later generations’ opportunities to live, learn and explore deserve protection.** [Hume’s Treatise, Book 3](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/3/1/1)

From that starting point, there are several ways I would choose to act:

1. **Reduce avoidable suffering.** Before making a decision, ask who might be harmed and what that harm would mean for the people or other sentient beings affected.
2. **Do work that later generations can build on.** Leave knowledge and methods they can understand, use and improve.
3. **Protect what would be hard to rebuild.** Protect human life and the conditions that sustain living, teaching and cooperation.
4. **Keep our understanding open to revision.** Reconsider our judgments when new experience calls them into question, and leave room for others to disagree and raise questions.
5. **Teach in ways that help others develop their own abilities.** Help them learn to judge for themselves and use what they have learned to take on new problems.

These practices can come into conflict. When a project intended to benefit people in the long term causes suffering now, we need to ask who bears the cost and whether there is a less harmful way forward. I discuss how disagreements like these arise in [*Where Do Ethical Disagreements Come From?*](../framework/methods/ethical-disagreement-map.md).

### Why carry good forward?

Looking back, we tend to notice first what was successfully passed down. This essay itself draws on earlier generations’ discoveries, records, teaching and cooperation. We can look back on this path at all because it has lasted to the present. Using the history that survivors experienced to estimate risk can lead us to underestimate disasters that would leave no observers. This is what survivorship bias asks us to consider. [Study of observation selection effects](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.2010.01460.x)

The knowledge we can still inherit shows that some acts of protection worked. In any particular disaster, we still have to ask what survived, what was lost and what people did. Those concrete choices show how goodwill can produce something that later generations can inherit.

### How resilience can accumulate

Cells can repair some damage, bodies can maintain their temperature as the environment changes, and immune systems can defend against many threats. These are examples of structures maintaining or regaining function under disturbance. In human societies, writing, institutions and science allow another form of protection. People can study risks that have yet to materialise, discuss how to respond and act together in advance.

**When a way of protecting something can be preserved, learned and improved, the capacity to protect it can itself accumulate.** Resilience depends on conditions at each level. A structure that can withstand one kind of disturbance may cease to work under another.

Goodwill can also be cultivated through practice. In one study, participants reported more positive feelings in response to other people’s suffering after a specific form of compassion training. That is a change observed under training. How goodwill becomes a habit and leads to acts of protection remains a question about how people live and relate to one another. [Klimecki and colleagues’ study of compassion training](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nst060)

### Understanding can change what happens

What people understand about their own situation can change the situation itself. When a well-founded warning changes what people do, it can lessen the anticipated harm. In the 1970s, scientists warned that some chemicals could deplete the ozone layer. In 1987, countries adopted the Montreal Protocol. An international scientific assessment found that measures under the protocol reduced the abundance of controlled ozone-depleting substances and advanced the recovery of the ozone layer. [Montreal Protocol](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol) · [WMO and UNEP ozone assessment](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/executivesummary/)

This is one practical consequence of the move described in Section 4, from working within a system to reflecting on its framing. People realised that their own activities were changing the environment and used that knowledge to change what they did. Knowing that something would be hard to rebuild if it were lost can give us a reason to protect it in advance.

### What I choose to believe

I choose to believe that good can ultimately outweigh evil. I think of specific people across generations who cared for those around them and chose to protect something for people they would never meet. Some protected lives, some preserved records, and some taught students the skills and judgments they had learned. Their actions allowed some achievements to survive destruction and gave later generations a chance to pass that goodwill on.

I want my own choices to make what I believe more likely to come true.

> **We are a part of the universe that can understand its own situation. We can recognise risks, choose to protect the living seeds, and in doing so change what might happen next.**

## 8. Connections with earlier ideas

This essay follows a tradition of looking to cosmic evolution to understand existence and meaning. I want to ask how structures already in place can preserve the past and open up new possibilities in a world without a predetermined purpose, where building and destruction happen together. When people understand how their choices can affect that process, what responsibilities can they take on?

**On the question of whether nature has a predetermined purpose, Spinoza is especially close to this essay.** In the *Ethics*, he discusses how people project their own purposes onto nature and how an understanding of nature as a whole can affect their lives. In his system, everything in nature follows from necessity. I focus on the role of historical contingency in particular paths of evolution and envisage cycles with no predetermined endpoint. [Spinoza’s *Ethics*, Part I](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)

Sagan situates human understanding within the universe. Simon shows how stable intermediate structures allow work to survive an interruption. Merton studies how people change a situation by acting on their understanding of it. These ideas connect respectively with the essay’s discussion of understanding, protection and action. Further comparisons follow in the afterword.

## 9. Questions still open

Cosmic awakening remains an evolving set of ideas. Some questions are still open.

- Under what conditions does expansion produce enough new material for another round of condensation?
- What kinds of condensation can preserve the past and generate new capabilities?
- Why does a system grow rigid, and how can it reopen possibilities?
- How does history enter the future? Could the universe have a kind of memory?
- Are life, cognition and civilization contingent outcomes of cosmic evolution, or do they express a deeper process?
- What kinds of structures can be carried forward after destruction, and can the conditions for accumulation observed in human knowledge help us understand other levels?
- Could beings capable of reflecting on themselves also emerge elsewhere in the universe? Searches so far have yielded no confirmed signal of extraterrestrial intelligence within the range they cover. How much can we infer from those results? This is a question raised by the Fermi paradox. [Study of the coverage of searches for extraterrestrial intelligence](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-3881/aae099)

The framework aims to preserve this picture of ongoing change.

> **The world continually generates new possibilities. Some condense into structures that preserve the past, generate new capabilities and become the starting point for another round of possibilities.**

This idea is a seed. Every act of reflection, discussion, questioning and creation is part of cosmic awakening.

A separate study takes human knowledge as its subject and seeks to describe information condensation and expansion as a testable mechanism. The hierarchical causal–control hypothesis (HCC) starts with local systems and proposes testable hypotheses about how structures form, persist and generate new capabilities. See [The Hierarchical Causal–Control Hypothesis](../framework/foundations/hierarchical-causal-control.md).

## Afterword: A guide to earlier ideas

This essay grew out of my long-standing reflections on the universe, evolution and the meaning of life. As I wrote, repeated conversations with AI helped me recognise affinities between some of my ideas and those of earlier thinkers, so I brought their work into the discussion. My thinking has also been shaped by my education, reading and personal experience, and many of those influences are hard to trace. This guide records the connections I can identify today and explains which questions I want to pursue from there.

### 1. The alternation of aggregation and separation

- **Empedocles** uses the alternation of Love and Strife to explain aggregation and separation. From a similar picture of alternation, this essay asks how what comes together in one round leaves conditions for the next. [Empedocles’ fragments](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Fragments_of_Empedocles)
- **The *Book of Changes*, Appended Statements II**, depicts the waxing and waning of yin and yang through the succession of sun and moon, cold and heat. This essay uses condensation and expansion to ask how the structures that form through such alternations can accumulate. [Appended Statements II](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/易傳/繫辭下)
- **Herbert Spencer’s *First Principles*** sets the integration of matter and the accompanying dissipation of motion against disintegration. This distinction can be read alongside the essay’s discussion of how structures form, persist and break down. [Spencer’s *First Principles*](https://constitution.org/2-Authors/hs/first_prin.htm)

### 2. Cosmic evolution, complexity and consciousness

- **Teilhard de Chardin’s *The Phenomenon of Man*** connects complexity and consciousness with the Omega Point of evolution. This essay retains the link between complexity and consciousness in a picture of evolution that remains open-ended.
- **Alfred North Whitehead’s *Process and Reality*** approaches reality through process and becoming. God and the “initial aim” have a specific place in his system. This essay considers formation, persistence and change within a philosophical picture without a predetermined purpose.
- **Carl Sagan’s *Cosmos*** presents human beings as a way for the universe to know itself. This essay then asks how people’s understanding of their own circumstances changes their actions and what might happen next. [The Library of Congress Sagan archive](https://www.loc.gov/static/collections/finding-our-place-in-the-cosmos-with-carl-sagan/about-this-collection/overview.html)
- **David Christian’s *Maps of Time*** places cosmic, biological and human history on the same long timescale and considers the conditions under which new levels emerge. This essay asks how the structures that have already formed can be preserved and used. [University of California Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/maps-of-time/epub-pdf)
- **Eric Chaisson’s *Cosmic Evolution*** uses the rate of energy flow per unit mass to compare the complexity of different systems. This offers a way to make quantitative comparisons. The present essay uses information condensation and expansion to pose philosophical questions.
- **Ray Kurzweil’s *The Singularity Is Near*** calls the stage in which intelligence expands into the universe “The Universe Wakes Up.” This essay uses that expression to ask how knowing ourselves relates to what we do.

### 3. Information and the universe

- **John Archibald Wheeler** explores the relation between information and physical reality through *It from Bit*.
- **Seth Lloyd** approaches the universe through computation and information processing.
- **Stephen Wolfram** studies how simple programs can produce complex behaviour.

These are lines of thought I cited in the 2025 essay. Following the thread of information, this essay asks how information expands, condenses into structures and, through those structures, changes the conditions for the next round of evolution.

### 4. Nature without a predetermined purpose and the question of meaning

- **Spinoza’s *Ethics*** discusses in Part I how people project purposes onto nature, and in Part V the intellectual love of God. This essay draws on the first discussion to identify teleological readings and uses the comparison to clarify why it attends to both regularities in nature and the historical conditions of evolution.
- **Ursula Goodenough’s *The Sacred Depths of Nature*** looks for awe, belonging and ethical concern in the natural world as described by science. This essay asks how those concerns take shape in what people choose to leave behind and protect. [Oxford University Press](https://academic.oup.com/book/52725)
- **Jean-Paul Sartre** emphasises how people shape themselves through their choices. This essay also approaches meaning through choice and places that capacity within the evolution of life and cognition.
- **Deborah Kelemen’s** research found that children were more likely than adults to explain some features of natural things in terms of what they are for. This offers a line of inquiry into why people seek explanations in terms of purpose.

### 5. Structure, selection and contingency

- **Ilya Prigogine** studies how flows of energy can give rise to ordered dissipative structures far from equilibrium. **Jeremy England** examines self-replication and dissipation from the perspective of statistical physics. Their work provides scientific background on order in systems with energy flows.
- **Herbert Simon’s *The Architecture of Complexity*** uses the watchmakers’ parable to show how stable intermediate assemblies allow complex things to be built in stages and work to resume after interruptions. This helps make sense of why knowledge sometimes survives a break in transmission and what a living seed can do.
- **Stephen Jay Gould’s *Wonderful Life*** and **Simon Conway Morris’s *Life’s Solution*** emphasise historical contingency and convergence, respectively. This essay distinguishes between structures that may recur under similar conditions and paths of evolution shaped by particular histories.
- **Lee Smolin’s** cosmological natural selection and **Roger Penrose’s** conformal cyclic cosmology explore inheritance and cosmic cycles from different directions. They can be read alongside the conjecture about two layers of information.
- **Scott Aaronson, Sean Carroll and Lauren Ouellette** give a concrete example of complexity rising and then falling in their study of the coffee automaton.

### 6. Meaning and ethics

- **Robert Cummins** analyses the function of a part through its contribution to an enclosing system. This can be read alongside the essay’s idea of a place in evolution.
- **Thomas Nagel** examines the subjective character of animal experience. **Jeremy Bentham and Peter Singer** bring animals’ capacity to suffer into ethical discussion. These lines of thought bear on what feels good or bad to a being and on reducing avoidable suffering.
- **David Hume** draws attention to the passage from what is to what ought to be done. This essay states the values from which it proposes conditional directions for action.
- **Hans Jonas’s** ethics of responsibility and **Aldo Leopold’s** land ethic draw attention to the long-term consequences of action for later generations and the living community. They bear on doing work others can build on and protecting what would be hard to rebuild.
- **Karl Popper’s** fallibilism and idea of an open society bear on keeping our understanding open to revision.
- **Robert K. Merton’s** self-fulfilling prophecy shows how people’s understanding of a situation can change it. This essay examines the reverse possibility, in which people who take a warning seriously may prevent the forecast harm.
- **Milan Ćirković, Anders Sandberg and Nick Bostrom** call attention to the “anthropic shadow” and ask how the survival of observers limits the estimation of some catastrophic risks.
