> This is a research note. It retains the exploratory process and conversational tone; its views and references will continue to be revised.

I consider this a crucial question in our current research. If the “cognitive disequilibrium” stage has a systematic weakness, the learning cycle may fail to sustain itself even with a good Generator, Framework and course design.

My central judgment is that cognitive disequilibrium is a valuable learning trigger, but neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for learning.

In other words:

- Facts that contradict expectations may stimulate curiosity.
- They may instead cause confusion, anxiety or irritation, or be dismissed as exceptions.
- Even curiosity does not guarantee correct understanding, lasting memory or transfer.

We therefore need to separate two questions:

First, how can students develop the urge to know an answer?

Second, under what conditions can that urge become effective, sustained, cumulative learning?

We previously focused on the first while designing some mechanisms for the second. The conditional relationship between them now deserves investigation.

## 1. The key research finding: disequilibrium does not necessarily upgrade a framework

One result warrants close study.

The 2023 *Studies in Science Education* review, “Response of Science Learners to Contradicting Information,” specifically examines responses to information conflicting with prior understanding.

A central conclusion is that merely creating cognitive conflict, without support for further thought or an intelligible alternative explanation, is generally not an efficient method of conceptual change.

ERIC

Consider a Brazilian student who expects verbs to vary with person and tense.

The teacher says Chinese does not work that way.

Several responses are possible:

**Cognitive disequilibrium:** Chinese verbs do not change?

**Avoidance:** That is strange; I will just memorize it.

**Local patch:** Chinese is a special exception.

**Framework upgrade:** Languages can use different solutions.

We want the third kind of response following the initial disequilibrium, but cannot assume every student will naturally reach it.

A 2024 meta-analysis covering 218 studies and 18,051 students also found substantial overall benefits from conceptual-change strategies, including cognitive conflict, but significant variation in effects. Implementation conditions matter.

Wiley Online Library

This gives a research-based rationale for placing a more fundamental Framework before cognitive disequilibrium in lesson one.

Students must not only see that an explanation is insufficient; they must see a path toward a better one.

But recognizing that “all languages are reasonable” is still insufficient. Students also need to understand why a particular expression works under particular constraints.

## 2. Eight major risks in the method

The following table pressure-tests the method using the literature and our current language-learning experiments.

| Potential problem | Why it arises | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Insufficient knowledge to generate predictions | Beginners do not know the possibilities; predictions become guesses | Provide a minimal knowledge core, basic facts and a few examples |
| 2. Excessive disequilibrium discourages exploration | New information cannot be connected to existing knowledge | Control difficulty and make a possible solution direction visible |
| 3. Anomalies are treated as exceptions | A strong existing Framework makes local repair easier than reconstruction | Establish common underlying principles, then compare implementations |
| 4. A question arises but is not pursued | Curiosity and motivation depend on interest, competence and personal goals | Connect questions to students’ concerns and provide achievable challenges |
| 5. Students infer an incorrect rule | A few examples support several competing explanations | Supply counterexamples, necessary rules and reliable feedback |
| 6. A concise framework becomes too abstract | A Generator explaining everything may predict nothing specific | Require testable, discriminating predictions |
| 7. Understanding does not become fluency | Understanding, memory and automaticity have different requirements | Add repeated retrieval, spaced practice and application |
| 8. Outcomes depend on guidance quality | Teachers may overcue, misjudge level or give incorrect explanations | Specify guidance rules, record actual reasoning and adjust prompts |

We have discussed the first three. I want to examine risks four, six and seven because they may limit the method’s scope.

## 3. The weakness most worth watching: disequilibrium is not curiosity

This is one assumption I think we need to revise.

George Loewenstein’s 1994 information-gap theory suggests that recognizing what one knows and lacks can produce curiosity to fill the gap.

But recognizing ignorance does not necessarily create a desire to know.

DOI

Consider three Brazilian students asked why Chinese verbs do not inflect.

Student A finds it interesting and seeks an explanation.

Student B thinks, “That is the Chinese rule; I will remember it.”

Student C thinks, “I have not learned basic vocabulary. Why investigate something so complicated?”

The same disequilibrium produces different learning behaviors.

### An important condition: the gap must be intelligible and tractable

### A possible relationship between prior knowledge and curiosity

This is a conceptual illustration, not measured data. It represents an inverted-U relationship often discussed in research: wholly unfamiliar or already very familiar material may stimulate less information-seeking than material that is partly understood but still contains a gap.

PubMed Central (PMC)

This fits your emphasis on guidance that narrows the search space.

I would add a condition:

Students must not only locate the question; they must believe further effort could resolve it.

Educational research on self-determination theory likewise emphasizes autonomy, competence and relational support in motivation.

ScienceDirect

The effectiveness of disequilibrium therefore depends on difficulty, interest, teacher support and perceived prospects of success.

## 4. A second fundamental weakness: a minimal Generator may not generate specific enough knowledge

This may be the method’s main epistemological risk.

Your minimal language-learning Generator says:

“Languages share the purpose of conveying meaning and implement it differently under their respective constraints.”

That is valuable.

But it only tells students that several reasonable implementations exist.

It cannot uniquely derive:

Why does Chinese generally use subject–verb–object order?

Why do some nouns use the classifier <span lang="zh-Hans">本</span> (*ben*, as with books), while others use <span lang="zh-Hans">张</span> (*zhang*, as with sheets)?

Why is one expression natural in modern Chinese while another is only marginally understandable?

These involve language-specific convention, history and usage.

Deriving possibilities from first principles is not uniquely deriving the possibility actually adopted.

A Generator that is too broad can retrospectively explain anything.

For example:

“Because language must be understood, every word order meets communicative needs.”

That accommodates nearly every order while hardly predicting which one a language uses.

This weakens its scientific value.

I do not suggest abandoning abstraction, but imposing a requirement:

A useful Generator should narrow the possibility space and state which facts would require revision.

This matches your emphasis on falsifiability, counterexamples and model revision.

## 5. A third fundamental weakness: understanding a mechanism is not fluent ability

Suppose the Brazilian student genuinely understands the different generative patterns of Chinese and Portuguese after lesson one.

They can explain word order, time expressions and some grammatical structures.

Yet rapid Chinese speech may remain wholly unintelligible.

Why?

Language learning involves at least several tasks:

**Conceptual understanding:** why an expression works and how rules connect.

**Knowledge retention:** vocabulary, characters, pronunciation, collocations and many specific facts.

**Skill automaticity:** rapid comprehension, formulation and natural speech.

Our disequilibrium method fits the first task particularly well in theoretical terms.

The second and third cannot be achieved solely by continually generating disequilibrium.

The major 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues and a 2021 meta-analysis of learning techniques support broad benefits from retrieval practice and spacing for long-term memory.

Sage Journals+1

Disequilibrium can help students discover, understand and revise rules. Once understood, much learning time should shift toward practice and application rather than more conflict.

Otherwise we may produce students who explain Chinese grammar well but cannot use Chinese well.

This does not reject the Generator; it defines its job.

## 6. A fourth weakness: students may think they understand when they do not

We emphasize explaining many phenomena through concise principles.

But psychology has an established finding called the illusion of explanatory depth.

People often think they understand a system deeply, then discover how incomplete their understanding is when asked to explain its workings.

PMC

This directly cautions our Generator approach.

After lesson one, a student might say:

“I understand! All languages solve shared communication tasks!”

That statement is correct.

Yet they may still fail to explain differences among five unfamiliar Chinese sentences.

Accepting an elegant unifying idea is different from mastering a generative mechanism that predicts facts.

Course evaluation must therefore go beyond asking, “Do you understand?”

Provide new problems and observe reasoning, prediction and error correction.

## 7. Where is this method most suitable?

I distinguish three levels of applicability. This is a preliminary judgment from the research and our framework, not a direct cross-domain experimental result.

| Learning type | Preliminary assessment | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scientific concepts, causal mechanisms and physical principles | Very suitable | Testable predictions and explanations exist |
| Economics, historical mechanisms and complex systems | Promising for exploration | Causal models can be compared, but competing explanations and uncertain evidence must be controlled |
| Mathematical concepts, modeling and problem solving | Very suitable | Existing structures generate questions, derivations and counterexamples |
| Foreign-language grammar and expressive structure | Suitable | Reveals structures but does not replace conventional knowledge |
| Vocabulary, characters and factual memory | Supporting role | Memorization, practice and retrieval remain necessary |
| Pronunciation, motor skills and response automaticity | Supporting role | Demonstration, real-time feedback and extensive practice remain central |

The method may suit conceptual reconstruction more than every stage of learning.

Once an adequate model exists and the task is greater speed, fluency and accuracy, continually creating disequilibrium is not the most efficient option.

## 8. Revise the method without overturning it

I do not suggest adding another complex theory.

Clarify the roles:

**Framework / Generator:** supplies shared tasks, known structure and basic constraints, narrowing the search space.

**Prediction and disequilibrium:** reveals gaps and anomalies in current understanding.

**Guidance and feedback:** supplies alternatives and necessary facts, supporting revision.

**Tests in new situations:** checks transferable understanding.

**Practice and accumulation:** turns understanding into stable knowledge and ability.

When needed, new situations generate the next predictions and questions.

Two changes follow.

First, disequilibrium need not occur in every learning round. Discovering common patterns or successfully solving a new problem can also sustain learning.

Second, the cycle may appropriately switch to practice, application and consolidation rather than continually seeking anomalies.

This avoids turning one useful mechanism into the only method all learning must follow.

## 9. An experiment that genuinely tests the core hypothesis

Before expanding the course, ask:

Does cognitive disequilibrium add learning benefits to our Generator method?

Use four groups of ordinary beginners, keeping teaching time, content and feedback quality as similar as possible.

| Group | First-principles framework | Deliberately designed predictions and cognitive conflict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: Conventional rule teaching | No | No |
| B: Framework teaching | Yes | No |
| C: Prediction-conflict teaching | No | Yes |
| D: Complete method | Yes | Yes |

If B clearly outperforms A, the underlying Framework has value.

Only if D outperforms B is there evidence of an additional effect from disequilibrium given the framework.

If D does not outperform B, we must acknowledge that a unifying framework and good guidance, rather than deliberate disequilibrium, may do most of the work.

Measure transfer to new problems, retention, question quality and willingness to continue immediately after learning, days later and over longer intervals—not only immediate answers.

This design is informative because it permits the core hypothesis to be rejected.

## 10. My overall assessment

The pressure test identifies a core worth retaining and an assumption to revise.

Retain:

First build a generative Framework that supports grounded expectations; then use appropriate guidance and feedback to make new facts opportunities for better understanding.

Revise:

Do not make cognitive disequilibrium the sole engine of learning.

Goals, interests, success, imitation, practice and other mechanisms can also drive learning. Disequilibrium is particularly useful when existing cognition cannot explain a new phenomenon.

A stricter principle for our method would be:

The goal is not continuous confusion but continual improvement in explanation, prediction and problem solving. Disequilibrium is one means to that end.

One deeper question remains.

We define disequilibrium as a mismatch between expectation and fact.

Must students discover an error to become strongly curious?

Suppose a student successfully derives an unfamiliar Chinese expression and discovers that the reasoning is right. They may become excited and want to explore further.

Could the competence and motivation produced by successful prediction be as important as disequilibrium from failed prediction—or more important for some learners?

That may be another basic mechanism we should investigate.

I now understand the distinction you emphasized. My earlier evaluation confused learning a language itself with learning how to understand and explore a language.

Your course explicitly addresses the latter.

The Genotype–Phenotype analogy separates those levels.

## 1. What we teach is the Generator, not all its instances

**Genotype · underlying generative mechanism**

Language conveys meaning.

Shared tasks, constraints, available expressive means and their relations.

Implemented under the conventions and conditions of particular languages:

Chinese — a specific rule system.

Portuguese — a specific rule system.

Uzbek — a specific rule system.

**Phenotype · concrete manifestations**

Vocabulary, sentences, characters, specific pronunciations and expressive habits.

Many instances requiring gradual exposure, observation and memory.

This is a structural analogy, not a claim that a fixed Generator uniquely determines every linguistic phenomenon. Each language also has historical conventions and its own rules.

For course design, however, the distinction is clear.

How a tone system uses pitch to distinguish meaning is structural knowledge worth understanding; which tone a particular character takes is a specific fact to learn.

We prioritize the former. The enormous number of facts does not require a short course to cover all of them.

## 2. A short course on learning how to learn languages

A Brazilian student completing the course may know only a dozen Chinese words, write few characters and lack spoken fluency.

By ordinary language-course standards, proficiency remains low.

By your objective, however, they may have gained something more valuable:

Knowing how to begin exploring an unfamiliar Chinese phenomenon independently.

For example, they encounter <span lang="zh-Hans">两本书</span> (“two books,” with *ben*) and <span lang="zh-Hans">三张纸</span> (“three sheets of paper,” with *zhang*).

They may not know all uses of the classifiers.

But they can reason from the shared task:

“The numeral already expresses quantity. Why is another word needed? What information does it supply, and what determines it?”

They can observe more examples, form hypotheses and test them.

They no longer require a teacher to state every rule because they know how to search for rules.

That is the intended ability.

## 3. Reconsidering disequilibrium’s role

We discussed weaknesses such as confusion, reluctance to ask questions and memorizing exceptions.

At the Genotype level, a more fundamental point appears:

Disequilibrium is not the course content. It is a mechanism for discovering, constructing and revising a Generator.

The method’s sequence should be:

1. Reconstruct the Framework so students stop treating their native language as the standard for all languages.
2. Supply a minimal Generator and necessary constraints, explaining shared problems and possible solutions.
3. Use guidance to form predictions and appropriate disequilibrium, letting students discover shortcomings themselves.
4. Practice revising and extending understanding, establishing an exploration path they can continue independently.

Later vocabulary study, pronunciation practice and character learning can follow that path. They need not dominate this short course.

## 4. This changes evaluation criteria

This distinction should be fixed clearly.

I previously proposed vocabulary retention, fluency and long-term retention.

Those matter for a complete language course but are not the principal measures for your short course.

More appropriate measures are:

| Ability | Measurement |
| --- | --- |
| Revising the prior framework | Does the student still treat other languages as exceptions to native-language rules? |
| Understanding the Generator | Can they explain different ways of accomplishing the same communicative task? |
| Narrowing the search space | Can they propose a few grounded explanations for an unfamiliar structure? |
| Active exploration | Can they design contrasting examples, request useful information and test guesses? |
| Independence from the course | Can they apply the method to untaught phenomena? |

The last is especially important.

It directly matches the goal: limited teaching time should support long-term independent exploration.

Initial teacher-generated questions do not invalidate this objective. The question is whether students gradually understand why those questions were asked and apply the thinking in new situations.

## 5. An important implication

If the objective is understanding a Generator, the course need not be limited to Chinese.

Two or three very different languages could serve as teaching material.

The research object is not Chinese, Portuguese or Uzbek as such, but:

How human language forms diverse, intelligible and comparable solutions under shared tasks and differing constraints.

Specific languages reveal that deeper structure.

This also explains why lesson one begins with multiple expressive possibilities inside the native language.

The aim is not to teach a few linguistic facts first, but to let students experience a move from surface Phenotype differences to Genotype mechanisms.

With this way of thinking, a new phenomenon may prompt “How is this generated?” rather than only “How should I memorize this rule?”

I would therefore state the course objective as:

Through sufficient but precise guidance in as little time as possible, help learners construct a minimal, revisable and transferable Generator so they can independently observe, reason, question and extend their understanding after the course.

That is the core hypothesis to test.

It returns the research to the original objective: not another language textbook, but how minimal initial knowledge and guidance might enable continued generation of understanding.

This discussion is directionally sound and **moves one level upward**: from “learning Chinese” to “acquiring a Generator that can keep developing.” It fits the original aim of enabling new understanding from minimal initial knowledge.

## What is valuable

**1. It separates structure from facts.** How pitch distinguishes meaning is understandable structure; a character’s particular tone is a fact to retain. This restates our distinction between derivable structure and conventional parameters, adding a curricular conclusion: the limited course takes responsibility for the former.

**2. Disequilibrium returns to its proper role.** It is a **mechanism** for constructing and revising the Generator, not content to teach. Concerns such as confusion and treating anomalies as exceptions become operating conditions to manage, rather than automatic refutations of the method.

**3. The evaluation table improves on my earlier indicators.** Beyond question-type proportions, it adds:

- **Narrowing the search space:** proposing a few grounded explanations for an unfamiliar structure. This addresses your Turkish-exercise concern: “I can make infinitely many predictions.”
- **Independence from the course:** applying the method to untaught phenomena.

**4. The course need not be tied to Chinese.** Its object is how shared tasks and different constraints generate different linguistic solutions; particular languages supply instances.

## What needs adding or revising

**1. Distinguish target language from native language.**<br>
The course need not be restricted to Chinese, but it does not follow that it is independent of students’ native language. **Predictions require a specific framework, and the native language is the ready-made starting one.** The Generator may be general while the entry point is customized. That is why lesson one differs for Brazilian and Uzbek students: the core is shared, but the native-language material generating expectations differs. Two or three contrasting languages can be a later extension; the first entry point should remain the learner’s own language.

**2. Transfer of “learning how to learn” is the main hypothesis, not an established outcome.**<br>
Far transfer from learning-to-learn approaches has repeatedly disappointed in educational history. A famous case is the idea that Latin trains general thinking, substantially challenged by Thorndike over a century ago. The chief risk is that students learn classroom analysis without using it during actual language learning.

The table measures **metacapacities**, but omits a downstream measure: **after the short course, do students learn real Chinese material faster?** Bransford and Schwartz (1999) call this “preparation for future learning”: assess transfer through subsequent learning, not only immediate problem solving. Have a control group and the course group learn the same new material—classifiers or <span lang="zh-Hans">了</span> (*le*), for example—and compare speed and accuracy of understanding. This directly tests the central claim.

**3. Designing contrasting examples is not yet taught in lesson one.**<br>
The table expects students to design comparisons and test guesses. But in the Turkish exercise, you initially noticed only sentence-final verbs and changing endings. I supplied the comparison separating *okudum* and *okudun*. Even a strong thinker may not spontaneously find minimal contrasts. If this is a target ability, teach it explicitly and gradually remove prompts until students design their own comparisons rather than relying on teacher-planted contrasts.

**4. Motivation is missing.**<br>
A Brazilian student who learns only a dozen words may feel they learned nothing and leave. That does not contradict the objective, but every lesson should let students **use the Generator to say or understand something new**, making metacapacity gains tangible through concrete progress.

## In one sentence

The objective is well chosen: **the course delivers a Generator, not a body of language facts.** To make that objective credible, add three things: the native language as the entry point for predictions, explicit teaching of comparison design, and the downstream test of faster subsequent learning.
