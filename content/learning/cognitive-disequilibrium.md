**Cognitive disequilibrium is a valuable trigger for learning, but it should not be treated as necessary for all learning or sufficient for effective learning.**

An observation that conflicts with an expectation may prompt curiosity, but it may also lead to confusion, avoidance or classification of the new fact as an exception. Even curiosity does not guarantee accurate understanding, lasting memory or transfer.

Two questions therefore need distinguishing: how learners discover a question worth pursuing, and how that inquiry becomes effective, sustained and cumulative learning. This article examines their conditional relationship through a short course intended to help learners understand and explore languages. The teaching arrangements and evaluation design below are working proposals; the additional effectiveness of the complete method has not been established.

<a id="course-objective"></a>

## 1. The course objective: a method for continuing to explore languages

The short course focuses on helping learners observe unfamiliar linguistic phenomena, propose grounded explanations, and test and revise their understanding. Completing it does not mean mastering a language, acquiring a large vocabulary, pronouncing accurately or speaking fluently.

Here, Framework means an initial structure for organising questions. Generator means a generative understanding that connects shared tasks, available expressive means and constraints, supporting explanations and predictions. It must do more than offer a broad slogan: it should narrow the possibility space and identify facts that would require revision.

For example, languages convey meaning and may use different expressive means. This starting point supports comparison, but cannot uniquely derive Chinese word order, the classifier used with a particular noun, or whether an expression sounds natural. These also involve language-specific conventions, history and usage.

Genotype and Phenotype can serve as a structural analogy: the former corresponds to shared tasks, constraints and generative relations; the latter to particular words, sentences, pronunciations and expressive habits. This is not a biological mechanism claim, nor does it imply that a fixed Generator uniquely determines every linguistic phenomenon.

How a tone system uses pitch to distinguish meaning is a structure that can be explored; the actual tone of a particular character is a fact to learn. The course can prioritise the former while supplying the basic facts needed to understand it. Subsequent vocabulary, pronunciation and expressive practice remain necessary.

**The central objective is to use limited, precise guidance to help learners construct a revisable, extensible Generator, enabling continued observation, reasoning, questioning and learning after the course.** Transfer to subsequent learning remains a hypothesis to test.

<a id="disequilibrium-and-curiosity"></a>

## 2. How cognitive disequilibrium gives rise to questions

In this article, cognitive disequilibrium refers to a gap in understanding when observations do not match existing expectations. It differs from cognitive bias in general and is not identical to curiosity.

For example, someone familiar with Portuguese may expect verb forms to change with person and tense. Encountering Chinese reveals that this expectation cannot simply be carried over. Possible responses include:

| Response | Possible manifestation |
| --- | --- |
| Identifying a gap and investigating it | Examining the basis of the expectation and how information such as time and person is expressed |
| Avoidance | Finding the difference too difficult and memorising the current form |
| Local repair | Treating Chinese as a special exception while retaining the overall framework |
| Framework revision | Comparing different ways languages accomplish the same communicative task |

These are illustrative possibilities, not classroom experimental findings. Noticing a difference does not automatically produce the last response. Guidance, basic facts and alternative explanations that can be compared are conditions to consider.[1](#ref-1)

The same knowledge gap may appeal differently to different learners. Its relationship to current goals, its comprehensibility and the visibility of a path for further inquiry may all affect subsequent behaviour.[3](#ref-3) [4](#ref-4)

Disequilibrium is also not the sole entry point for sustained learning. Successfully explaining a new phenomenon or correctly predicting an unfamiliar expression may likewise motivate further inquiry. The conditions under which successful prediction or prediction failure is more effective still need comparison.

<a id="teaching-cycle"></a>

## 3. From an initial framework to independent inquiry

The teaching sequence can be used with the [Framework-Driven Learning Cycle](./framework-driven-learning.md). Its components have distinct functions:

| Stage | Main function | What to observe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Establish an initial Framework | Start with familiar material and recognise the native language as one implementation among several | Can learners explain where their expectations came from? |
| Supply a minimal Generator and necessary facts | Organise shared tasks, expressive means and constraints to narrow the search space | Can learners propose a few grounded explanations rather than arbitrary guesses? |
| Form predictions and observe differences | Make gaps in understanding concrete | Can learners identify exactly where expectation and observation differ? |
| Guidance, comparison and feedback | Supply discriminating examples, test explanations and revise the framework | Can learners identify observations that support or challenge an explanation? |
| Test in new situations | Check whether the method remains usable | Can learners handle untaught material with progressively fewer prompts? |
| Practice, application and accumulation | Turn understanding into more stable knowledge and practical ability | Are retention, accuracy and ability to use the knowledge recorded separately? |

Disequilibrium is one mechanism for discovering and revising a Generator. Deliberate conflict need not occur in every round. When an explanation is sufficiently clear and speed and accuracy become the main tasks, teaching can shift towards practice and consolidation.

### The target language may vary; the starting point must consider the native language

The course may use Chinese, Portuguese or other languages as material. The shared research object is how languages develop different expressive means under shared tasks and differing constraints.

Cross-language material does not make the learner's native language irrelevant to the starting point. Predictions require an existing framework, and the native language is often a readily available one. The underlying objective may be shared while the material used to form expectations and comparisons is adapted to prior knowledge.

### Comparison design must be taught, and prompts gradually withdrawn

When two expressions differ, learners may notice different endings or word order without knowing how to identify the condition responsible. Designing minimal contrasts should be taught explicitly: hold other conditions as constant as possible, vary one condition under investigation and observe how the expression changes.

Teaching can proceed from demonstrations to partially completed contrasts and then to independent selection of discriminating examples. Teachers can supply the initial questions while still pursuing independent inquiry. The key is whether learners can later explain why a comparison matters and use it independently in a new setting.

<a id="risks-and-boundaries"></a>

## 4. Eight risks and boundaries of applicability

| Potential problem | Why it arises | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Insufficient knowledge for predictions | Beginners do not know the possibilities and guess randomly | Supply a minimal knowledge core, basic facts and a few examples |
| 2. Differences are too large and inquiry stops | New information is difficult to connect with existing knowledge | Adjust task difficulty and provide intermediate steps |
| 3. Anomalies become exceptions | Local repair may be easier than rebuilding the framework | Compare implementations and test whether the original framework explains new material |
| 4. A question does not prompt further inquiry | Interests, goals, perceived competence and expected effort differ | Relate questions to learning goals and ensure each lesson produces tangible progress in understanding or use |
| 5. Incorrect rules are inferred | A few examples can support competing explanations | Supply counterexamples, necessary facts and reliable feedback |
| 6. The Generator is too broad | It accommodates every outcome retrospectively without discriminating explanations | Require specific predictions and record facts that would force revision |
| 7. Principles are understood but use is not fluent | Conceptual understanding, factual memory and skill automaticity are different tasks | Define the roles of the short course and later training; include retrieval, spacing and practical application |
| 8. Performance depends too heavily on the teacher | Hints, prompts or teacher-completed reasoning conceal independent ability | Record prompt levels, withdraw support progressively and test untaught material separately |

### Evaluate understanding, memory and automaticity separately

Explaining structural differences between Chinese and Portuguese does not imply understanding rapid Chinese speech. Conceptual understanding, factual accumulation and skill automaticity each require appropriate training and assessment.[6](#ref-6) [7](#ref-7)

The course does not aim to cover all linguistic facts, so every unknown word should not count as a course failure. Equally, describing the course as methodological does not remove the need to test its effect on subsequent learning.

### Accepting a unifying idea is not mastering a generative mechanism

Learners may repeat that different languages solve shared communicative tasks while remaining unable to explain unfamiliar sentences. Assessment should require concrete explanation, prediction, comparison and error correction rather than relying on a feeling of understanding. Research on the illusion of explanatory depth provides relevant grounds for this distinction.[5](#ref-5)

### Distinguish applicability by learning task

| Learning task | Work this method can address | Additional requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scientific concepts, causal mechanisms and physical principles | Construct predictions, compare explanations and counterexamples | Reliable observations, disciplinary knowledge and feedback |
| Economics, historical mechanisms and complex systems | Compare mechanisms and their conditions | Address competing explanations and uncertain evidence |
| Mathematical concepts, modelling and problem solving | Generate questions and derivations from existing structures | Correct proofs, practice and methodological boundaries |
| Foreign-language grammar and expressive structure | Identify relations between expressive means and constraints | Language-specific conventions and authentic usage material |
| Vocabulary, characters and fixed facts | Organise and connect material to learn | Memory, retrieval and spaced practice |
| Pronunciation, motor skills and rapid responses | Understand tasks and feedback | Demonstration, timely feedback and extensive practice |

This table is a preliminary analysis of task allocation, not an experimental finding about teaching effectiveness across fields. Disequilibrium may suit stages that require revised understanding; that does not establish suitability for every learning task.

<a id="evaluation"></a>

## 5. Evaluating the course

The course primarily assesses whether learners can continue exploring, rather than only counting words retained immediately. Main measures can include:

| Ability to assess | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| Revising an existing framework | Do learners still treat other languages as exceptions to native-language rules, and can they explain how an expectation needs revision? |
| Understanding the Generator | Can they explain different ways languages accomplish the same communicative task and identify the explanation's limits? |
| Narrowing the search space | Can they propose a few grounded, comparable explanations for an unfamiliar structure? |
| Active inquiry | Can they design contrasting examples, request useful information and test guesses? |
| Reduced dependence on prompts | Can they independently use the method with untaught phenomena? |
| Preparation for subsequent learning | Does learning the same new material show improvements in understanding speed, accuracy and later retention? |

The last measure connects classroom methodological performance with learning after the course. Bransford and Schwartz's preparation-for-future-learning perspective emphasises observing transfer through the speed or quality of subsequent learning, rather than only immediate unaided problem solving.[8](#ref-8)

For example, the course group and a comparison group could study untaught classifiers or sentence patterns under the same conditions, with comparisons of understanding, learning time and delayed performance. Analysing a classroom example alone does not establish lasting or cross-domain transfer.

<a id="experimental-design"></a>

## 6. Testing whether disequilibrium adds an effect

One question is whether learning gains mainly come from the initial framework and good guidance, or whether deliberately arranged prediction and cognitive-conflict activities add an effect. A four-group design could examine this:

| Group | Explicit initial-framework instruction | Deliberately designed prediction and conflict activities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: Conventional rule teaching | No | No |
| B: Framework teaching | Yes | No |
| C: Prediction-conflict teaching | No | Yes |
| D: Complete arrangement | Yes | Yes |

Here, no framework instruction means no explicit framework component as proposed in this article; it does not mean learners have no existing framework. Teaching time, content, material and feedback quality should be kept as comparable as possible, with appropriate group assignment and records of implementation differences and learners' starting points.

- B versus A estimates the increment from explicit framework instruction under the relevant conditions.
- D versus B estimates whether prediction and conflict activities add an effect when framework instruction is already present.
- C versus A, and D versus C, help examine whether the increment changes with the framework condition.

If D does not show an advantage over B, deliberate disequilibrium cannot simply be claimed as necessary; sample size, measurement and implementation also need consideration. Even when differences appear, the groups primarily test teaching arrangements. Those differences alone do not confirm a particular psychological mechanism.

Immediately after learning, days later and over longer periods, separately observe transfer to new problems, subsequent learning, retention, question quality and willingness to continue. Record teacher prompts during tasks so that teacher-completed reasoning is not counted as learner independence.

<a id="conclusion"></a>

## 7. What to retain and what to test

The core worth retaining is an initial framework that supports grounded expectations, followed by observation, comparison, guidance and feedback that make new facts opportunities to revise understanding.

The questions to test are whether the course supports independent inquiry after completion, whether deliberate disequilibrium adds an effect, and which tasks, starting knowledge and guidance conditions support that effect.

**The course aims to improve explanation, prediction and problem solving. Disequilibrium is one means; goals, interests, successful experiences and practice can also support learning.**

<a id="related-research"></a>

## 8. Relationship to existing research

### Conceptual change and contradictory information

Potvin's review examines science learners' responses to contradictory information, providing background for analysing cognitive conflict and conceptual change.[1](#ref-1)

Pacaci, Ustun and Ozdemir's meta-analysis includes 218 studies and 18,051 students, examining strategies such as cognitive conflict, cognitive bridging and ontological category shift in science education. It reports a positive overall effect and differences between studies.[2](#ref-2) These findings concern the included science-education studies. They do not directly establish the effectiveness of the sequence or complete language-course design proposed here.

### Information gaps and learning motivation

Loewenstein explains curiosity in terms of perceived gaps in knowledge or understanding.[3](#ref-3) Ryan and Deci's self-determination theory discusses autonomy, competence and relatedness support in motivation.[4](#ref-4) This article accordingly treats noticing a gap and being willing to investigate it separately. The specific guidance arrangements proposed here still require their own tests.

### Perceived understanding, practice and future learning

Rozenblit and Keil show that perceived understanding can exceed actual explanatory ability.[5](#ref-5) Dunlosky and colleagues' review, and Donoghue and Hattie's meta-analysis, provide relevant evidence for practice testing and distributed practice.[6](#ref-6) [7](#ref-7) Bransford and Schwartz offer a transfer perspective for assessing preparation for subsequent learning.[8](#ref-8)

These studies support distinctions among learning tasks and assessment approaches, rather than directly validating every teaching arrangement in this article. The integration, allocation of course responsibilities and four-group design retain the status of working proposals.
