Read alongside [Concept Packaging and the Constraints of Characters](../language/concept-packaging-and-characters.md). Use the [framework-driven learning cycle](./framework-driven-learning.md) to record predictions, discrepancies and revisions.

## A one-sentence diagnosis

Beyond acquiring vocabulary and rules, native Chinese speakers learning English should watch for three habits that their first-language experience may encourage. This is a training perspective, not a claim that every learner has identical difficulties:

- **Connections rely on word order, meaningful characters and listeners filling in gaps.** English often requires endings, function words and syntactic positions to mark roles, time, number and other grammatical information.
- **Concepts are assembled from transparent characters, encouraging Chinese-style expansion of complex relational concepts.** English can package large concepts in opaque labels. Learn their participant structure and collocations, not just a Chinese equivalent.
- **Attention is distributed across characters, with speech unfolding segment by segment.** In English production practice, identify the subject and main verb first, then organize the remaining information around this skeleton.

Learning guidance therefore has two tasks: understand and accept a different encoding system, then change habits through sustained practice.

## 1. Accept a different encoding system, rather than merely “more rules”

Divide the task into representing concepts and connecting them. Chinese often uses word order and independent morphemes; English also makes extensive use of inflectional endings, function words and syntactic structure. Both languages have lexical and grammatical components; this is not an absolute division.

The troublesome little elements—-s, -ed, the, a, it, there, to, of and for—belong to English’s “connecting layer.” They principally perform grammatical functions and should not be understood as meaningless. Their functions also differ. Relate them to situations and communicative tasks rather than memorizing isolated rules.

## 2. Six training priorities

| What to practice | Why, in terms of our proposed account | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Establish the skeleton first | Identify the subject and main verb, then complements and modifiers; do not transfer a Chinese topic word for word. English also has premodification, so this is not a rule that “all modifiers come afterward.” A common challenge is committing to the subject and main verb at the outset. | Before speaking, produce “subject + one verb,” then add material. Recast a Chinese topic: <span lang="zh-Hans">这里很冷</span> (“here is very cold”) → *It is cold here*. |
| 2. Check required grammatical information | Attend to subjects, number, tense and determination required in the relevant positions; necessity and form depend on construction and context. | In clearly contextualized exercises, temporarily remove cues such as *yesterday* and check verb forms. Describe the same picture while checking required plurals and articles. |
| 3. Hear the connecting layer | Function words are often reduced, and -s/-ed can be missed in connected speech. Give attention to these less salient sounds. | Do listening-discrimination exercises focused on reduced function words and endings; deliberately exaggerate them during shadowing. |
| 4. Learn verbs with their role slots | Chinese and English participant structures and prepositional collocations do not correspond one to one: compare Chinese constructions for marrying someone or apologizing to someone. Literal transfer invites errors. | Learn *marry + someone*, not the word alone. Compare *marry someone*, *contact someone* with *listen to music*, *wait for me*. Record actual additions and omissions. |
| 5. Package an event in a noun | English can compress an event into a noun placed in subject position; Chinese often unfolds the event in successive segments. | Decompress *response* as “a reaction to something”; compress <span lang="zh-Hans">政府反应太慢</span> (“the government responded too slowly”) into *the government’s slow response*. |
| 6. Learn abstract words as whole labels, with their sounds | Many abstract English words cannot be guessed directly from appearance. Combine roots, affixes, context, collocations and pronunciation. Spelling–sound correspondences have both patterns and exceptions. | Learn words in sentences and collocations, not through single Chinese equivalents. Learn pronunciation–spelling correspondences early. Teach roots as an analogy to “English characters”: *bio-* suggests life, and *-tion* commonly forms nouns. This is a teaching analogy, not equivalence between linguistic units; it lets the habit of inferring meaning from components operate at the morpheme level. |

## 3. Practice that changes habits

This section applies retention, feedback and habit-formation ideas to practice design. Sequence and dosage should be adjusted through learning feedback.

1. **Stabilize a small core before opening up variation.** Depending on the starting level, practice a small set until it is reasonably stable and retrievable in time: skeleton rules; *it* and *there*; -s/-ed/the; and twenty or thirty common relational verbs with their role slots. A stable lower layer permits freer combinations above it. Expanding before stabilizing the core builds new material on an unstable foundation.
2. **Target errors that do not prevent understanding.** Articles and -s can be wrong while the listener still understands, so everyday communication may provide no explicit feedback. These errors can become entrenched. Precisely because no one points them out, learners must check deliberately.
3. **Correct early, before errors become premises.** Repeated errors can become the basis of later expressions and grow harder to change. Use frequent, timely self-checking rather than correcting a large accumulated batch.
4. **Alternate practice and consolidation.** In a hot phase, speak extensively in situations and under time pressure. In a cool phase, record errors and correct forms, then review them before the next practice. Practice without recording loses gains; recording without practice does not create habits.
5. **Use the learning cycle throughout.** The first language supplies an initial rule source, supplemented by a small teacher-provided core framework. Predict first, then compare with real English. Correct predictions accelerate learning; incorrect ones become occasions to accept another representation and generate the next question.

## 4. What not to do

- Do not treat English grammar only as isolated rules to memorize; return rules to communicative tasks and contexts.
- Do not translate Chinese sentence structures word for word, especially constructions with open slots such as <span lang="zh-Hans">对……负责</span> (“be responsible for…”).
- Do not memorize abstract words through a single Chinese equivalent.
- Do not confuse understanding a rule with being able to use it. Understanding enables acceptance; changing habits requires repeated contextual use under time pressure.

## Scope

These suggestions address long-term learners seeking real command of English. Those who only want a few common expressions can use existing materials.

For implementation, divide section 2 into six practice units: attempt an expression, record what was actually omitted, then re-express it with the target construction. Focus on one or two errors at a time and check independent use in new situations, rather than success only on the original item.
