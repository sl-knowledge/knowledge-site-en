“I’ve been eating less and exercising every day. I lost three pounds the first week and another pound the second week. Then my weight stopped falling. Some days it even went up a pound.”

Why does this happen? And if two people both lose five pounds (about 2.3 kilograms), have they lost the same thing?

Early in weight loss, changes in glycogen and water can make the number on the scale fall quickly. Later, the actual energy deficit can shrink even if the eating and exercise plan stays the same. To see how both changes work, start with energy balance.

## 1. The body is like a tank with water flowing in and out

Imagine a tank with water flowing in at one end and out at the other. If more water flows in than out, the level rises. If more flows out than in, it falls. When the two flows match, the level stays steady.

The body has a similar energy balance. We get energy from food and spend it in several ways:

- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)**. Even at rest, the body spends energy on circulation, breathing, brain activity and maintaining body temperature.
- **Thermic effect of food (TEF)**. Digestion, absorption and processing food require energy.
- **Non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT)**. Standing, walking around and doing chores all use energy outside deliberate exercise.
- **Exercise energy expenditure**. Deliberate physical activity such as running, swimming, cycling or resistance training also uses energy.

Even a day spent sitting still takes substantial energy. When the body takes in more energy than it spends, some of the surplus can be stored. When intake falls short of expenditure, the body draws on its stores.

**Energy deficit = energy expenditure − energy intake.**

Write energy intake as $EI$ and energy expenditure as $EE$. When the actual deficit is positive, the body’s stored energy must decrease to make up the difference. That is conservation of energy. What does the body store, and how does it draw on those reserves?

## 2. How does the body store energy? Glycogen and fat

The body digests and absorbs food and uses the nutrients for energy. Carbohydrates can be broken down into glucose, an important fuel for cells. The brain normally uses a substantial amount of glucose. We go for stretches without eating, so the body stores some energy for later use.

### Glycogen is an accessible short-term reserve

The body links glucose molecules together to make glycogen, stored mainly in the liver and skeletal muscle. Between meals, the liver can break down glycogen and release glucose to help maintain blood glucose. During exercise, muscles can draw on their own glycogen stores for energy.

Glycogen is stored with water. Kevin Hall and colleagues used approximately 2.7 grams of associated water per gram of glycogen in their dynamic model of body weight. This is a model value and can vary with conditions. Changes in glycogen stores can therefore produce noticeable changes in scale weight through associated changes in water. [Hall et al., model appendix](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/-/media/Files/BWP/Hall_Lancet_Web_Appendix.pdf)

### Fat is an important long-term reserve

When energy intake stays above the body’s needs, one way to store the surplus is as triglycerides in adipose tissue. Fat stores a great deal of energy per unit of mass. When the body draws on its reserves, it can break down stored fat and use it for energy.

Glycogen is an accessible short-term reserve, while fat provides a larger long-term store. The body often uses several fuels at once. Protein can also contribute under some conditions. The proportions depend on diet, activity and the body’s state.

## 3. Where do glycogen and fat belong in body weight?

In a two-compartment model of body composition, total body weight is divided into two parts.

**Fat mass (FM)** is the total mass of fat in the body. Much of it is in adipose tissue under the skin and around internal organs. When fat loss is appropriate for someone’s health, the aim is to reduce excess fat stores. Fat also serves important physiological functions, including cushioning organs and contributing to hormonal regulation.

**Fat-free mass (FFM)** includes everything else, including skeletal muscle, organs, bone, water and glycogen. Both glycogen and its associated water belong to FFM.

Writing total body weight as $W$ gives

$$
W = \mathrm{FM} + \mathrm{FFM}
$$

Two pounds of FFM loss might come largely from glycogen and water. Fat-free mass, lean tissue, lean mass and skeletal muscle mass are related measurements with distinct definitions.

This is why a drop in weight during weight loss can reflect changes in both fat and fat-free mass.

## 4. Why does the scale fall quickly at first and then slow down?

Suppose someone weighs 190 pounds (about 86 kilograms), reduces calorie intake and substantially cuts carbohydrate intake. If you take glucose-lowering medication, read the [medical note below](#medical-note) before making a major change to your diet.

Glycogen stores may fall in the first few days, and their associated water may fall quickly too. These changes show up in FFM. If an actual energy deficit continues, the body also draws on energy reserves including fat.

Both FM and FFM can fall at the beginning, and the rapid change in glycogen and water can make weight loss look especially large. A four-pound drop (about 1.8 kilograms) in the first week might include a substantial amount of glycogen, water and other components.

The initial rapid changes in glycogen and water usually diminish. As long as a deficit remains, the body still has to draw on its energy stores. Over time, the aim is for more of the loss to come from excess fat while preserving muscle. The mix can differ between the early and later stages.

This answers the question at the beginning. The same five-pound loss can come mostly from glycogen and water in one instance and mostly from fat in another. An equal change on the scale can represent different changes in body composition.

## 5. The planned energy deficit and the actual deficit can differ

Return to the tank. Suppose daily energy intake and expenditure originally matched at 2,300 kilocalories. Someone plans to cut 300 kilocalories from food and spend an additional 200 through exercise. On paper, that gives a daily deficit of 500 kilocalories.

Cooking oil, sauces and snacks are easy to underestimate. Exercise can leave someone tired enough to move less during the rest of the day. It can also make someone hungrier and lead to eating more. A planned deficit of 500 kilocalories could turn out to be 300.

The **planned deficit** is what a change in eating and activity is intended to create. The **actual deficit** is the difference between what the body really spends and what it takes in. The actual deficit determines how stored energy changes.

As weight falls, the body may need less energy to maintain its tissues and move around. Sometimes the drop in energy expenditure is larger than changes in body weight and composition alone can explain. This additional reduction is called **adaptive thermogenesis**. [Hall et al., 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880593/)

Appetite can rise too. In 2016, David Polidori and colleagues used weight changes from a trial in people with type 2 diabetes to estimate that each kilogram of weight lost increased the feedback driving energy intake by roughly 100 kilocalories per day. This was a group-level model estimate under specific trial conditions. The researchers inferred changes in intake from changes in weight. [Polidori et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5108589/)

Even with the same eating and exercise plan, the actual energy deficit can gradually shrink. Energy is still conserved. The body’s feedback changes how much energy comes in and goes out.

## 6. Why can weight plateau? Two possibilities

### A temporary plateau on the scale

If an actual deficit continues, the body’s energy stores still have to decrease. Changes in water, glycogen or the contents of the digestive tract can temporarily hold scale weight steady. For instance, eating more carbohydrate can increase glycogen and associated water, temporarily hiding a loss of fat.

### Energy intake and expenditure come back into balance

The initial deficit can shrink as appetite increases, intake rises or expenditure falls. If intake and expenditure stay close to balance, stored energy stops falling on an ongoing basis. The original plan may cease to produce further weight loss. A modelling study by Thomas and colleagues showed that occasional departures from planned intake can produce an earlier plateau. Several causes can contribute in a particular case. [Thomas et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25080458/)

Scale weight can hold steady because water temporarily masks fat loss or because the actual deficit is close to zero. The two effects can overlap, and a few days of weigh-ins give little basis for telling them apart.

## 7. Do you need to eat less as your weight falls?

**It depends.** Take a separate example. Suppose someone at 190 pounds (about 86 kilograms) spends 2,300 kilocalories a day and takes in 1,800, a daily deficit of 500. After some weight loss, suppose their daily expenditure falls to 2,200. If intake stays at 1,800, the deficit falls to 400. The body still draws on its energy stores, though weight loss may slow.

Intake can also rise as appetite increases. In this example, if daily intake rises to 1,900 or 2,000 kilocalories, the actual deficit shrinks to 300 or 200. The numbers illustrate how intake and expenditure together change the deficit.

**We can adjust what and how much we eat and how we move. The actual energy deficit emerges from those choices and the body’s feedback.** Daily expenditure varies with body composition, activity and metabolism, and intake can only be estimated. **Feedback control** means using what we observe to guide adjustments. When driving, we watch where the car is going, steer a little if it drifts, and see where it goes next. Changes to eating and activity can work the same way.

Start with an arrangement you can sustain. Weigh under similar conditions, compare seven-day averages and look at the trend over several weeks. Periodic waist measurements and changes in energy, strength and everyday function can provide more context. Weight and waist measurements can show a trend. On their own, they cannot tell us how much fat was lost or establish that the deficit is close to zero.

- **The trend fits your goal and you feel well.** You can stay with the current arrangement. A slower pace alone is no reason to eat less.
- **There is little change for several weeks.** Review eating, activity and other factors first, then decide whether a small adjustment makes sense.
- **Weight is falling too fast, or energy, strength or everyday function worsens.** Check whether the plan has become too demanding. Seek medical assessment for marked dizziness, persistent fatigue or other symptoms.

For adults for whom weight loss is appropriate, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention describes a gradual pace of about one to two pounds (0.45 to 0.9 kilograms) per week. At 190 pounds, that is roughly 0.5% to 1% of starting weight. This gives a sense of scale. Several weeks of measurements and how the body is functioning also matter. [CDC, Steps for Losing Weight](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/losing-weight/index.html)

Very rapid weight loss can also increase the risk of gallstones. [NIDDK, Dieting & Gallstones](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/gallstones/dieting)

As you approach the goal, you can slow the pace based on how your body responds and move towards habits you can sustain over time.

## 8. What happens to muscle during weight loss?

### Why can muscle also decrease during weight loss?

Think back to the water tank in Section 1. This time, the tank holds protein in skeletal muscle. The body continually makes new muscle protein and breaks down existing protein. Subtract the amount broken down from the amount made over a period of time to see whether muscle protein has accumulated or declined.

**Net change in muscle protein = amount made − amount broken down**

Eating less can reduce protein intake as well. Amino acids from food supply the building blocks for muscle protein. During short-term energy restriction, one trial found lower synthesis at rest in the fasted state. Another trial, in middle-aged men with overweight or obesity, matched protein intake across groups. Over ten days, average muscle protein synthesis in the two energy-restricted groups did not differ detectably from the energy-balance group. [Areta et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24595305/) · [Kouw et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39418832/)

If less water flows in, the level can fall even if water flows out at the same rate. In a short trial, Hector and colleagues found lower muscle protein synthesis with no detectable change in the rate of breakdown. Some amino acids released by muscle can also be used to make glucose. That is one possible use for the amino acids. Net change in muscle protein still depends on the difference between synthesis and breakdown. [Hector et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28899879/) · [Consoli et al., 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2240206/)

Resistance exercise challenges muscle with a load that stimulates synthesis. In the short trial under energy restriction, resistance exercise reduced the drop in muscle protein synthesis. This helps explain why it matters what building blocks are available, what loads the muscles work against, and how large the energy deficit is. [Hector et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28899879/)

Starting fat mass can also influence what makes up the weight lost. The Forbes relationship describes a tendency for fat-free mass to make up a larger share of weight loss when starting fat mass is lower. Fat-free mass includes water and glycogen, so this share alone cannot tell us how much muscle was lost. [Hall, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17367567/)

### What should we preserve while losing fat?

Other things worth protecting include strength, fitness, nutritional status and the ability to manage everyday life and work. A plan can be hard to sustain if it substantially impairs concentration during mentally demanding work or strength during physical work.

The tank suggests three things we can adjust. Adequate protein provides the building blocks for synthesis. Resistance training provides a load for the muscles to work against. Keeping the energy deficit from becoming too large leaves more room to support synthesis and everyday function. [Pasiakos et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23739654/) · [Hector et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28899879/)

Preserving strength and mobility matters especially in older age. In a weight-loss trial in older adults with obesity, groups that did resistance exercise retained more lean mass than the group that did aerobic exercise alone. [Villareal et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28514618/)

A meta-analysis in adults with overweight or obesity found that adding resistance training to a dietary intervention helped preserve fat-free mass, improve strength and increase fat loss. The groups did not differ significantly in total weight loss. [Binmahfoz et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40909191/)

The aim is to reduce excess fat while maintaining health, nutrition and physical function.

### What can protein powder help with?

Most people can get enough protein from everyday foods. Protein powder and other protein supplements can fill a shortfall. Resistance training and the size of the energy deficit also matter for preserving muscle during weight loss. Before using a supplement, consider whether you need it, how it fits into your total daily intake and what risks it may carry. **If you have chronic kidney disease, diabetes or other risk factors for kidney disease, talk to your doctor before increasing your protein intake.**

Further reading: [NIH guide to exercise supplements, protein section (for athletes and active people)](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-Consumer/) · [FDA 101: Dietary Supplements](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/fda-101-dietary-supplements) · [NIDDK guide to healthy eating with chronic kidney disease](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/kidney-disease/chronic-kidney-disease-ckd/healthy-eating-adults-chronic-kidney-disease)

## 9. Reaching a weight and maintaining it are two different tasks

Imagine two people who go from 190 pounds (about 86 kilograms) to 175 pounds (about 79 kilograms). One gets there by enduring hunger and strictly limiting food. The other changes what they eat, how they cook and how they move, and gradually adapts to those habits.

They reach the same weight, but staying there may take different amounts of time, effort and willpower and have different effects on everyday life. Call this the **long-term cost of maintaining the new weight**.

Feedback through appetite and energy expenditure can persist after weight loss, and weight can return. Eating and activity habits that are easier to keep up can make maintenance more sustainable. Reaching a weight and maintaining it while staying healthy and able to function are two questions worth considering from the start.

## 10. Put the model together

**A change in eating and activity → a change in the actual energy deficit → changes in energy stores and body composition → feedback in appetite and expenditure → a possible further change in the deficit.**

Energy conservation determines what happens to stored energy. Body composition tells us what contributes to a change in weight. Feedback in appetite and expenditure helps explain changes in pace and the possibility of a plateau. Observing those changes helps us decide whether to adjust eating and activity. The remaining question is whether the new state is healthy and sustainable.

### The equation behind energy balance

Section 3 divides total weight $W$ into fat mass and fat-free mass. If $E$ represents stored energy, then

$$
\frac{dE}{dt} = EI - EE
$$

If $E$ is measured in kilocalories and $t$ in days, $EI$ and $EE$ are metabolizable energy intake and energy expenditure in kilocalories per day. Fat mass and fat-free mass have different compositions and energy properties, so changes in body weight do not map directly to changes in energy stores.

As weight falls, expenditure $EE$ can decline and appetite feedback can push intake $EI$ back up. When actual intake and expenditure return to near balance and the body’s stores stop changing on net, the system can approach a new steady state. Weight loss therefore need not follow a straight line at a constant daily rate.

## In closing

Energy conservation explains why the body draws on stores. Body composition explains why a change on the scale can include glycogen, water, fat and other tissue. Feedback explains how the pace can change, and maintenance poses a further question about what habits the new state requires.

**The aim is to reduce excess fat while protecting health and normal function, and to reach a state that can be maintained over time.**
